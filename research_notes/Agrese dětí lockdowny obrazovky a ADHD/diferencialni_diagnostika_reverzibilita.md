## 1. Which conditions are recognized as ADHD mimics, and how do guidelines and diagnostic manuals tell clinicians to rule them out?

### Takeaway
Guidelines all say the same three things: ADHD must be established from multi-informant evidence in more than one setting, with childhood onset. The clinician must actively rule out or screen for alternative explanations (mood and anxiety disorders, trauma and toxic stress, sleep disorders including sleep apnea, learning and language disorders, substance use). Rating scales or neuropsychological tests alone are not enough. None of the guideline texts I retrieved names screen media as a differential diagnosis. The Czech psychiatric-society chapter on children could not be fetched; what could be seen of it focuses on treatment algorithms.

### Cited Findings

**AAP 2019 clinical practice guideline (USA)**
- Citation: Wolraich ML, Hagan JF, Allan C, et al. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Treatment of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder in Children and Adolescents. *Pediatrics* 2019;144(4):e20192528. DOI 10.1542/peds.2019-2528. PMID 31570648, PMC7067282. Scite: verified, no notices. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- KAS 2 (verbatim, Grade B strong recommendation): "To make a diagnosis of ADHD, the PCC should determine that [DSM-5] criteria have been met, including documentation of symptoms and impairment in more than 1 major setting (ie, social, academic, or occupational), with information obtained primarily from reports from parents or guardians, teachers, other school personnel, and mental health clinicians who are involved in the child or adolescent's care. The PCC should also rule out any alternative cause." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- KAS 3 (verbatim, Grade B): "the PCC should include a process to at least screen for comorbid conditions, including emotional or behavioral conditions (eg, anxiety, depression, oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorders, substance use), developmental conditions (eg, learning and language disorders, autism spectrum disorders), and physical conditions (eg, tics, sleep apnea)." The text adds that comorbid conditions "include but are not limited to learning disabilities, language disorder, disruptive behavior, anxiety, mood disorders, tic disorders, seizures, autism spectrum disorder, developmental coordination disorder, and sleep disorders." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- Adolescents: clinicians "must establish that an adolescent had manifestations of ADHD before age 12 and strongly consider whether a mimicking or comorbid condition, such as substance use, depression, and/or anxiety, is present"; "Certain substances, such as marijuana, can have effects that mimic ADHD"; adolescents may feign symptoms to obtain stimulants. "Trauma experiences, posttraumatic stress disorder, and toxic stress are additional comorbidities and risk factors of concern." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- Sub-threshold ("problem level") presentations: "Also consider that these symptoms may suggest other problems that mimic ADHD." Parent training in behaviour management (PTBM) is recommended without needing a diagnosis. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- Age floor: "there is insufficient evidence to recommend diagnosis or treatment for children younger than 4 years (other than [PTBM])". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- Testing: "The use of neuropsychological testing has not been found to improve diagnostic accuracy in most cases." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)

**NICE NG87 (UK)** [secondary summary: nice.org.uk could not be fetched]
- Diagnosis "should only be made by a specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified healthcare professional with training and expertise in the diagnosis of ADHD". It must rest on "a full clinical and psychosocial assessment … [including] behaviour and symptoms in the different domains and settings of the person's everyday life", a developmental and psychiatric history, observer reports and a mental state assessment. Symptoms must meet DSM-5 or ICD-10 criteria and "be pervasive, occurring in two or more important settings including social, familial, educational and/or occupational settings". A diagnosis "should not be made solely on the basis of rating scale or observational data". Primary care "should not make the initial diagnosis or start medication". — [NICE QS39 statement 1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs39/chapter/Quality-statement-1-Confirmation-of-diagnosis); [NG87 recommendations page](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)
- Caution: the search summary also quoted "significant impairment in at least one domain after environmental modifications have been implemented and reviewed" as a diagnostic threshold. I could not confirm where this phrase sits in NG87 (it may be a medication-initiation criterion, not a diagnostic one), so it should not be cited as a diagnostic rule without checking. — [NG87 recommendations page](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations)

**DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR criteria** [secondary reprints; the APA text was not accessible]
- ≥6 symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity for children up to 16 years (≥5 at 17+), lasting ≥6 months. "Several inattentive or hyperactive-impulsive symptoms were present prior to age 12." Several symptoms are present in two or more settings. There is clear evidence of interference with functioning. Symptoms are "not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., mood disorder, anxiety disorder, dissociative disorder, personality disorder, substance intoxication or withdrawal)". — [DSM-5 criteria reprint (Masaryk Univ. course file)](https://is.muni.cz/el/ped/jaro2019/SPp207/um/DSM5_DiagnosticCriteria_ADHD.pdf?lang=en); [CDC Diagnosing ADHD](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/diagnosis/index.html)

**ICD-11 6A05** [secondary mirrors of WHO text]
- Persistent pattern (≥6 months) of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity with direct negative impact on functioning. Significant symptoms present before age 12. Symptoms "evident across multiple situations or settings (e.g., home, school, work, with friends or relatives)". "Symptoms are not better accounted for by another mental, behavioural, or neurodevelopmental disorder and are not due to the effect of a substance or medication." Presentations are coded 6A05.0/.1/.2/.Y/.Z. — [CISMeF ICD-11 6A05](https://www.cismef.org/page/detail/en/ICD11_CA_821852937); [Find-A-Code 6A05](https://www.findacode.com/icd-11/code-821852937.html)

### Inferences
- Formally, the anti-misclassification tools are the same across AAP, NICE, DSM-5(-TR) and ICD-11: (a) onset in childhood (several symptoms before 12), (b) pervasiveness in ≥2 settings with multi-informant documentation, and (c) an exclusion clause ("not better explained by"). None of the retrieved texts operationalizes "environmental cause" (e.g., screens, sleep loss, family stress) as a formal exclusion test. It is covered only implicitly by "rule out any alternative cause" (AAP KAS 2).
- A text search of the AAP 2019 full text found no mention of screen or digital media in the diagnostic recommendations. Sleep apnea and sleep disorders, trauma and toxic stress, substance use (marijuana), anxiety, depression, learning and language disorders are named explicitly.
- Sensory impairment (vision, hearing) and early deprivation did not appear in the AAP passages I extracted. I have no retrieved guideline text that names them as mimics (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- NICE NG87 verbatim text, DSM-5-TR official wording, and the Czech children's guideline full text could not be retrieved: WebFetch to nice.org.uk, cdc.gov, postupy-pece.psychiatrie.cz, publications.aap.org, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and nursinginpractice.com all failed with "ENOTFOUND – host outside the session's allowed network domains". The AAP guideline was read through the PubMed MCP full-text tool instead.
- No European paediatric ADHD guideline (e.g., European clinical guidelines for hyperkinetic disorder, German AWMF S3) was retrieved. An Elicit search for "European clinical guidelines hyperkinetic disorder … differential diagnosis" returned only Tourette guidelines and general reviews.
- No retrieved guideline text was found on vision/hearing screening, or on early institutional deprivation as an explicit ADHD differential.
- Whether DSM-5-TR (2022) changed any ADHD criterion wording relative to DSM-5 (2013) was not verified.

## 2. Named constructs for screen- or environment-induced attention/emotion dysregulation: who proposed them, peer-review status, evidence, criticism, retractions

### Takeaway
None of the named constructs is a validated diagnostic entity. "Electronic Screen Syndrome", "Attention Deficit Trait", "digital dementia" and "popcorn brain" come from a blog and book, a management-magazine essay, a popular-science book, and a media interview respectively, with no controlled validation. "Screen dependency disorders" is a peer-reviewed review label (Sigman 2017) that is mainly an umbrella over internet/gaming addiction. "Virtual autism" has one peer-reviewed causal-argument review (Harlé 2019) and case reports. The best quantitative synthesis (Ophir 2023) found the screen–ASD association non-significant after publication-bias correction, and a 2026 *Autism* commentary argues the term should be replaced. No retractions or expressions of concern were found for any of these papers.

**Attention Deficit Trait (ADT): Edward M. Hallowell**
- Citation: Hallowell EM. Overloaded circuits: why smart people underperform. *Harvard Business Review* 2005;83(1):54–62, 116. PMID 15697113 (no DOI). — [Source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15697113/)

### Inferences
- The constructs fall into three groups. (1) Practitioner-coined, non-peer-reviewed labels with self-reported clinical series: ESS, ADT, popcorn brain. (2) A popular-science thesis rebutted in peer-reviewed literature: digital dementia. (3) Peer-reviewed narrative/causal-argument reviews without prospective controlled validation: virtual autism (Harlé), SDD (Sigman). None has operational diagnostic criteria tested for reliability or validity in the retrieved literature.
- None of these terms appears in the DSM-5/ICD-11 criteria texts retrieved for Q1. Of the related behaviours, ICD-11 codifies only gaming disorder; that coding is from background knowledge, not from a retrieved source.
- The recovery claims shared by ESS and virtual autism ("symptoms resolve after weeks to months of screen removal") rest on uncontrolled clinical observation and case reports. Controlled evidence on screen reduction (Q3) is short-term and does not test those specific claims.

## 3. Do ADHD-like symptoms, irritability or aggression decrease after environmental change, and on what timescale? Is there evidence of persistence ("scarring")?

### Takeaway
Experimental and quasi-experimental evidence shows that environmental inputs can shift attention, emotional lability and aggression in either direction on timescales from minutes to months:
- Sleep restriction or extension (one night to one week) moves inattention and emotional lability.
- A 20-minute park walk acutely improves concentration in children with ADHD.
- A 2-week family screen fast reduced SDQ total difficulties (d = 0.53, mostly internalizing).
- A 6-month TV/game reduction curriculum reduced peer-rated and observed verbal aggression.
- Treating sleep problems or sleep-disordered breathing improved ADHD ratings over 3–12 months.

The evidence has clear limits. No RCT shows that screen reduction normalizes clinically significant ADHD in children. The best OSA RCT (CHAT) found no effect on objectively tested attention. Extreme early deprivation (more than 6 months of institutional care) produces inattention/overactivity that persists into adulthood and does not respond to foster-care placement, which is consistent with a sensitive period.

### Cited Findings

**Screen reduction**
- **SCREENS trial, mental-health secondary analysis.** Schmidt-Persson J, Rasmussen MGB, Sørensen SO, et al. "Screen Media Use and Mental Health of Children and Adolescents: A Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Clinical Trial". *JAMA Netw Open* 2024;7(7):e2419881. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.19881. PMID 38995646. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.19881)
  - Design: cluster RCT (randomized by family), prespecified secondary analysis; Southern Denmark (10 municipalities); enrolment June 2019 – March 2021.
  - Sample: N = 89 families, 181 children aged 4–17. Intervention mean age 8.6 (SD 2.7), control 9.5 (SD 2.5).
  - Intervention: 2 weeks with leisure screen use ≤3 h/week and smartphones/tablets handed over; 97 % compliant on objective tracking.
  - Measure: parent-reported SDQ, 21 days between assessments.

### Inferences
- **Time course, from the retrieved studies.**
  - Minutes: a 20-minute park walk acutely improved concentration (d ≈ 0.5–0.8).
  - One night to one week: sleep restriction raises inattention, but not hyperactivity, in healthy children (Fallone 2001/2005; Lundahl meta-analysis). ±1 h over a school week shifts teacher-rated emotional lability and restlessness (Gruber 2012).
  - Two weeks: a family screen fast reduced SDQ difficulties (mainly internalizing and prosocial) in non-clinical children.
  - Three to twelve months: sleep-intervention and adenotonsillectomy effects on ADHD ratings (Hiscock 3–6 months; Chervin 1 year); aggression reduction after 6 months of media reduction (Robinson).
  - Pandemic data: month-to-month tracking of symptom levels with lockdowns and reopenings, at the group-mean level.
- **Asymmetry of the evidence.** Sleep manipulations act mainly on inattention and emotional lability; hyperactivity is affected less consistently. Media-reduction trials show effects on aggression and internalizing problems, but no trial reports a clinically meaningful reduction in core hyperactivity/inattention in children with ADHD-level symptoms. "Environmentally reversible ADHD-like states" is therefore plausible for inattention and lability, and less established for full ADHD syndromes.
- **CHAT.** CHAT's null primary outcome shows that improved behaviour ratings after removing an environmental or physiological driver do not necessarily show up on objective attention tests. Parent ratings in an unblinded design may overstate change (SCREENS was also open-label).
- **Sensitive periods.** Persistence evidence (ERA, BEIP) concerns severe global early deprivation in infancy, not screen exposure. It shows that some environmentally caused ADHD-like phenotypes become resistant to later environmental improvement (insensitive to foster care, persistent to age 22–25). Whether heavy early screen exposure has any analogous sensitive-period effect is untested; extrapolating from institutional deprivation would be speculative.
- **Pandemic heterogeneity.** In the pandemic natural experiments, most children were stable and children with pre-existing neurodevelopmental conditions recovered least. A subgroup did improve during lockdown. Population-average "reversibility" therefore hides heterogeneity.

### Gaps
- No RCT was found that tests screen reduction against clinically assessed ADHD symptoms or ADHD diagnosis in children. The SCREENS hyperactivity/inattention subscale result was not reported in the text, and the trial excluded neurodevelopmental disorders.
- PATS primary neurobehavioural outcomes (mild SDB) were not retrieved.
- Robinson 2001 total N, and Gruber 2012 and Lundahl 2015 effect sizes, were not in the abstracts retrieved.
- No study directly testing "scarring" after childhood screen exposure (sensitive-period design) was found.
- Search notes: Consensus MCP was unavailable (free monthly quota exhausted, "resets on December 1"; all 3 queries failed). ## 4. How do clinicians distinguish ADHD from environmentally induced dysregulation in practice, and what are the misclassification risks in each direction?

### Takeaway
In practice, distinguishing ADHD from an environmentally induced state relies on four things:
- history: onset of several symptoms before age 12, and ideally before the suspected environmental exposure;
- pervasiveness across settings with multiple informants;
- active screening for alternative drivers (sleep, sleep-disordered breathing, trauma and stress, mood and anxiety, learning disorders, substance use);
- observing the response when that driver is addressed.

No validated tool or biomarker was found that separates "environmental" from "neurodevelopmental" ADHD-like presentations; the AAP says neuropsychological testing does not improve diagnostic accuracy in most cases. Both error directions are documented:
- Overdiagnosis: the Kazda 2021 scoping review, and relative-age effects driven by teacher ratings.
- Mistaken environmental attribution: children with ADHD or autism show more (problematic) screen use, and selection effects run from ADHD to media use. Heavy screen use can be a consequence or regulatory strategy of ADHD rather than its cause.

- Thorell 2022 (above): "children with ADHD symptoms appear more vulnerable to developing high or problematic use of digital media (i.e., selection effects)". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-022-02130-3)
- Hill 2024 (above): children with autism or ADHD "may be given access to greater screen time to help manage difficult behavior". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10578-024-01785-0)
- Ophir 2023 (above): children with ASD "may prioritize screen activities to avoid social challenges". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.46775)

**Diagnostic tools**
- AAP: neuropsychological testing "has not been found to improve diagnostic accuracy in most cases". DSM-based rating scales are the standard for collecting parent and teacher information. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2019-2528)
- NICE: diagnosis is not to be made "solely on the basis of rating scale or observational data". [secondary summary] — [Source](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs39/chapter/Quality-statement-1-Confirmation-of-diagnosis)
- Sleep: CHAT's baseline NEPSY attention scores were near the population mean despite OSAS, and polysomnographic severity did not predict neurobehavioural improvement in Chervin 2006. Measured sleep-disordered-breathing severity is therefore a weak discriminator. — [CHAT](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1215881); [Chervin 2006](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2005-1837)
- Preschool screening: PATS baseline analysis found primary snoring and mild OSA "difficult to distinguish without polysomnography". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamaoto.2023.3816)

### Inferences
- **Practical discriminators implied by the retrieved guidelines and studies.**
  - (a) Temporal order: documented symptoms before heavy exposure or before the stressor (DSM/ICD onset criterion; AAP adolescent guidance).
  - (b) Cross-setting consistency with independent teacher reports. Relative-age data show teacher ratings carry their own bias.
  - (c) Systematic screening for sleep restriction and sleep-disordered breathing, trauma/ACEs, family stress and parental distress (Co-SPACE), mood and anxiety, learning and language disorders, and substance use.
  - (d) A time-limited trial of environmental change (sleep intervention, screen reduction, PTBM) before or alongside diagnosis, especially for sub-threshold or preschool presentations, consistent with AAP's "problem level" and PTBM-without-diagnosis guidance.
  - These four steps are an inference: no guideline retrieved prescribes an environmental "challenge test".
- **Error toward overdiagnosis** is most likely in milder cases, young-for-grade children with teacher-driven referrals, children under 4–6 years (AAP: insufficient evidence below 4), and children with sleep or trauma burden.
- **Error toward underdiagnosis** is most likely when heavy screen use is read as the cause, given the documented selection effect (ADHD → more and problematic media use) and the reciprocal associations. A "screen fast" that does not normalize functioning should push toward, not away from, a full ADHD assessment. Removing screens can also remove a coping strategy and transiently increase dysregulation; this is plausible but not tested in the retrieved literature.
- Comorbidity is the norm (AAP: most children with ADHD meet criteria for another disorder), so "environmental cause" and "ADHD" are not mutually exclusive. Sleep problems in children with ADHD are themselves treatable contributors (Hiscock 2015).

### Gaps
- No validated instrument or biomarker for separating environmentally induced from neurodevelopmental ADHD-like presentations was found; none was named in the AAP text. A dedicated search for biomarker consensus statements was not run due to tool budget.
- No paediatric study of trauma or PTSD misclassified as ADHD with extractable numbers was retrieved: Weinstein 2000 abstract unavailable; the Spanish systematic review (Aguirre Sánchez 2022, DOI 10.21134/rpcna.2022.09.1.1) is qualitative.
- The Gosling et al. 2023 *Lancet Psychiatry* individual-participant-data meta-analysis on relative age and ADHD persistence (DOI 10.1016/s2215-0366(23)00272-9) and Park et al. 2026 *JAACAP Open* on directional associations between digital media use and ADHD (DOI 10.1016/j.jaacop.2026.07.005) were identified, but their abstracts were not retrievable.

## 5. Chronic irritability and outbursts: DMDD, Leibenluft's irritability research, and the relation to ADHD

### Takeaway
DMDD (DSM-5 2013) was created to stop chronic non-episodic irritability being diagnosed as paediatric bipolar disorder. Its criteria are demanding: ≥3 outbursts per week, persistent irritable mood, ≥12 months, onset before 10, minimum age 6, multiple settings. With all criteria applied it is uncommon (~1 % in school-age samples), it has poor test-retest reliability (κ = 0.25 in DSM-5 field trials), and it overlaps heavily with ODD and ADHD. In ADHD samples, 21.8 % (community, 6–8 years) to 31 % (clinic) meet DMDD criteria. Leibenluft's work places chronic irritability on a path to anxiety and depression, not bipolar disorder, with heritability of 40–60 %. ICD-11 chose an ODD "with chronic irritability" specifier instead of DMDD. Experts explicitly instruct clinicians to search for environmental causes (psychosocial stressors, maltreatment, family conflict, learning or communication disorders) before attributing outbursts to a disorder.

### Cited Findings
- **Axelson 2013, commentary on Copeland et al.** Axelson D. "Taking Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder Out for a Test Drive". *Am J Psychiatry* 2013. DOI 10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.12111434. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.12111434)
  - DMDD criteria: severe temper outbursts at least three per week; irritable or angry mood most of the day, nearly every day; duration ≥12 months with no more than 3 consecutive months symptom-free; minimum age 6; onset before 10; present in multiple settings.
  - Copeland's epidemiological data: nearly half of school-age youths had severe temper outbursts in the prior 3 months. The frequency criterion reduced prevalence ~7-fold to 6–7 %, the duration criterion to 1.5–2.8 %, and all criteria to ~1 %. In preschoolers, 81 % had severe tantrums, 18 % at least three times a week, and 3.3 % met all criteria except age.
  - Reliability and stability: DSM-5 field-trial test-retest κ = 0.25. Cumulative prevalence by age 16 was 4.4 %, four times the point prevalence.
  - Overlap: co-occurrence with ODD had odds ratios of 53–103; about 25 % of school-age youths with ODD met DMDD criteria.
  - Instruction to clinicians: "It is imperative for clinicians to search for potential causes of severe irritability in an individual child, whether it be psychosocial stressors, history of maltreatment, family conflict, learning or communication disorders, other axis I psychiatric disorders, or some combination."
- **Carlson & Pataki 2016.** Carlson GA, Pataki C. "Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder Among Children and Adolescents". *Focus* 2016. DOI 10.1176/APPI.FOCUS.20150039. DMDD symptoms "better explained by autism, posttraumatic stress disorder, separation anxiety, and dysthymia are exclusionary". DMDD "can co-occur with [ADHD], conduct disorder, and substance use disorder" and "preempts diagnoses of both oppositional defiant disorder and intermittent explosive disorder". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1176/APPI.FOCUS.20150039)
- **Leibenluft 2011, severe mood dysregulation.** Leibenluft E. "Severe Mood Dysregulation, Irritability, and the Diagnostic Boundaries of Bipolar Disorder in Youths". *Am J Psychiatry* 2011. DOI 10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.10050766. "Nonepisodic irritability in youths is common and is associated with an elevated risk for anxiety and unipolar depressive disorders, but not bipolar disorder, in adulthood." Verified, no notices. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2010.10050766)
- **Leibenluft 2017, World Psychiatry review.** Leibenluft E. "Irritability in children: what we know and what we need to learn". *World Psychiatry* 2017. DOI 10.1002/wps.20397. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20397)
  - Irritability is defined as "increased proneness to anger, relative to peers". Anger proneness peaks in the preschool period, declines thereafter, and rises modestly in adolescence.
  - Heritability of irritability is approximately 40–60 %.
  - Within ODD, the irritability dimension predicts later anxiety and depression, while the headstrong dimension predicts ADHD and conduct disorder.
  - ICD-11 includes an ODD "chronic irritability" specifier instead of DMDD.
  - "Considerable data indicate that stimulants reduce irritability in youth with ADHD."
- **Brotman 2017, translational model.** Brotman MA, Kircanski K, Stringaris A, Pine DS, Leibenluft E. "Irritability in Youths: A Translational Model". *Am J Psychiatry* 2017. DOI 10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16070839. Irritability is conceptualized as aberrant responding to frustrative non-reward (reward system) and aberrant approach responding to threat (threat system), involving prefrontal cortex, striatum and amygdala. Verified, no notices. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16070839)
- **Mulraney 2016, DMDD in community ADHD sample.** Mulraney M, Schilpzand EJ, Hazell P, et al. "Comorbidity and correlates of disruptive mood dysregulation disorder in 6–8-year-old children with ADHD". *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2016. DOI 10.1007/s00787-015-0738-9. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-015-0738-9)
  - Design: Melbourne, Australia, 43 schools; 179 children with ADHD confirmed by DISC-IV.
  - Results: 39 (21.8 %) had proxy DMDD. Among them, 89.7 % had ODD and 41.0 % any anxiety disorder. DMDD was associated with poorer self-control and more bullying.
  - Limitation: a proxy DMDD built from ODD items may inflate the overlap.
- **Eyre 2017, DMDD in clinic ADHD sample.** Eyre O, Langley K, Stringaris A, Leibenluft E, Collishaw S, Thapar A. "Irritability in ADHD: Associations with depression liability". *J Affect Disord* 2017. DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2017.03.050. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.03.050)
  - Design: UK clinics; 696 children with ADHD, mean age 10.9; semi-structured interview (CAPA).
  - Results: 91 % endorsed at least one irritable symptom; 3-month DMDD prevalence 31 %. Irritability and DMDD were associated with anxiety, depression and a family history of depression.

- **Dickstein 2009, lithium RCT in severe mood dysregulation.** Dickstein DP, et al. *J Child Adolesc Psychopharmacol* 2009. DOI 10.1089/cap.2008.044. 45 % (20/45) of youths with severe mood dysregulation were not randomized because of "significant clinical improvement during the [2-week] placebo run-in". - **Environmental modulation of irritability and aggression (from Q3):**
  - Teacher-rated emotional lability improved with ~27 min of extra sleep and worsened with ~54 min of restriction (Gruber 2012). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2012-0564)
  - Peer-rated and observed verbal aggression fell after 6 months of media reduction (Robinson 2001). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/archpedi.155.1.17)

### Inferences
- Chronic irritability is a cross-diagnostic dimension: common in ADHD, central to DMDD and ODD, and linked to later internalizing disorders. "Aggression or outbursts" in a child is not specific evidence for ADHD, DMDD or an environmental cause. The DMDD duration (≥12 months) and frequency criteria, and the expert instruction to search for psychosocial causes, mean that short-lived, context-bound outbursts (e.g., during a lockdown) do not meet DMDD criteria by definition.
- The high placebo run-in response in severe mood dysregulation (45 %) and the high base rate of severe tantrums in preschoolers (81 %) suggest that cross-sectional irritability ratings are unstable and sensitive to context. This supports re-assessment over time before labelling.
- ICD-11 (used in Czech practice) has no DMDD. Chronic irritability is coded via an ODD specifier, which matters for Czech clinicians translating DSM-based literature.

- No study was found that tests whether DMDD-level irritability remits after screen reduction or school return.
- The ICD-11 ODD chronic-irritability specifier text was not retrieved from WHO directly; it is stated in Leibenluft 2017.
