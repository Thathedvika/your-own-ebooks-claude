# COVID-19 lockdowns, school/preschool closures and reduced social contact: effects on children's behaviour, self-regulation and social development (evidence through Nov 2026)

Scope note: written 2026-11-12. Sources were found via PubMed, Scite (search + verify_papers), and WebSearch. Most full-text pages could not be opened because of network restrictions (see the search log under the last section's Gaps), so effect sizes come from abstracts, Scite full-text excerpts, or search-engine summaries of official pages. Scite `verify_papers` was run on about 55 DOIs. It found **no retractions or expressions of concern**. One paper has an erratum: Hawrilenko et al. 2021 ([erratum](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.32935)).

## Q1. What do meta-analyses and pre/post longitudinal studies show for externalizing, aggression/irritability, hyperactivity/inattention, emotional regulation and peer/social skills?

### Takeaway
Across meta-analyses and cohorts with pre-pandemic baselines, the most consistent deterioration was in **internalizing symptoms, well-being, loneliness and peer problems**. Changes in **externalizing/conduct problems were smaller and less consistent**. Several high-quality cohorts found no change or even a slight *improvement* in conduct problems, while others found small increases. Hyperactivity/inattention shows small but fairly consistent increases, mostly in parent reports. The largest pre-registered-style synthesis, a 2026 meta-analysis of 157 longitudinal studies of 0–12-year-olds, found increases in behavioural problems and declines in social-emotional skills from pre- to during-pandemic. There were no further declines across pandemic time points.

### Cited Findings

**Meta-analyses / systematic reviews**
- **Stacheder M, Oppermann E, Anders Y (2026).** "In the shadow of crisis: A systematic review and meta-analysis on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on children's social-emotional skills, behavioral adjustment, and psychological well-being." *Child Development*. DOI 10.1093/chidev/aacag090.
  - Design: meta-analysis of **157 longitudinal studies, 33 countries, children aged 0–12**.
  - Results: "significant declines in children's social-emotional skills and well-being, and increases in behavioral problems in studies comparing pre-pandemic and during-pandemic data. No further declines emerged in studies comparing multiple time points during the pandemic."
  - Pooled effect sizes and CIs were **not retrievable**: the publisher site was blocked and Scite had no indexed full text. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag090)
- **Madigan S et al. (2023).** "Changes in Depression and Anxiety Among Children and Adolescents From Before to During the COVID-19 Pandemic." *JAMA Pediatrics*. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.0846.
  - Design: 53 longitudinal cohorts, 12 countries, 87 estimates; 40,807 children pre-pandemic and 33,682 during.
  - Depression standardized mean change (SMC) **0.26 (95% CI 0.19–0.33)**; anxiety **0.10 (0.04–0.16)**.
  - Subgroups: females 0.32 (0.21–0.42); Europe 0.35 (0.17–0.53); mid–high income 0.35 (0.07–0.63).
  - Internalizing only; included as the benchmark against which externalizing changes look smaller. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.0846)
- **Newlove-Delgado T et al. (2023).** "Annual Research Review: The impact of Covid-19 on psychopathology in children and young people worldwide: systematic review of studies with pre- and within-pandemic data." *JCPP* 64(4):611–640. DOI 10.1111/jcpp.13716.
  - 51 studies, **only 4 rated high quality**. Heterogeneity limited meta-analysis.
  - Authors' summary: "a slight deterioration on some measures, overall, the findings were mixed, with no clear pattern emerging." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.13716)
- **Kauhanen L et al. (2023).** *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry*. DOI 10.1007/s00787-022-02060-0.
  - 21 longitudinal or repeated cross-sectional studies, 11 countries, >96,000 participants aged 3–24.
  - Most reported deterioration in adolescents (depression, anxiety, distress, loneliness). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-022-02060-0)
- **Alcon S et al. (2024).** "Effects of the COVID-19 Pandemic on Early Childhood Development and Mental Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Comparative Studies." *Psychology International* 6(4):62. DOI 10.3390/psycholint6040062.
  - Design: preschool children 0–6; 22,348 children from 16 countries; Cohen's d.
  - **SDQ** pooled changes, all with CIs crossing zero:
    - total d = −0.088 (−0.247 to 0.07)
    - hyperactivity d = −0.012 (−0.124 to 0.10)
    - peer problems d = −0.112 (−0.275 to 0.501)
    - prosocial d = −0.145 (−0.474 to 0.185)
    - emotional d = 0.07 (−0.044 to 0.184)
    - conduct problems: "negligible increase"
  - **CBCL** pooled changes:
    - emotional reactivity d = 0.344 (0.087–0.60)
    - anxiety/depression 0.464 (0.263–0.722)
    - withdrawal 0.42 (0.163–0.678)
    - attention problems **0.28 (0.024–0.536)**
    - aggressive behaviour **0.277 (−0.016 to 0.571; p = 0.206)**
  - The abstract describes "small increases in emotional symptoms and conduct problems", but the SDQ-based estimates do not exclude zero. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint6040062)
- **Rogers M, MacLean J (2023).** "ADHD Symptoms Increased During the Covid-19 Pandemic: A Meta-Analysis." *J Atten Disord* 27(8):800–811. DOI 10.1177/10870547231158750.
  - 18 studies (12 longitudinal, 6 retrospective), 6,491 participants, 10 countries.
  - Concludes there was an increase in ADHD symptoms. The pooled effect size could not be retrieved (full text restricted).
  - Six of the 18 studies used retrospective recall. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547231158750)
- **Dessain A et al. (2023).** *Neurosci Biobehav Rev*. DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105502.
  - Only **6 controlled longitudinal cohort studies** of youth with ADHD qualified.
  - Whether symptoms worsened more in the ADHD group or in controls depended on the symptom and the context. Youth with ADHD had more persistent symptoms. Quality was mostly low. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105502)
- **Viner R et al. (2022).** "School Closures During Social Lockdown and Mental Health, Health Behaviors, and Well-being…" *JAMA Pediatrics* 176(4):400–409. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5840.
  - 36 studies, 11 countries, 79,781 children and 18,028 parents; first wave only, with closures lasting 1 week to 3 months.
  - 25 studies (69%) found associations across "emotional, behavioral, and restlessness/inattention problems"; 18–60% of children scored above risk thresholds.
  - School-closure effects "could not be separated from broader lockdown measures." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5840)
- **Saulle R et al. (2022).** Second-wave review, *Epidemiol Prev*. DOI 10.19191/EP22.5-6.A542.089.
  - 12 studies: 2 uncontrolled before–after and 10 cross-sectional; 27,787 participants.
  - "Clear signal of increase" in mental-health problems with school closure or virtual instruction, but no meta-analysis was possible. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.19191/EP22.5-6.A542.089)

**Cohorts with pre-pandemic baseline (SDQ subscales and related)**
- **COPSY wave 1 vs BELLA (Germany).** Ravens-Sieberer U et al. *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2022;31:879–889. DOI 10.1007/s00787-021-01726-5.
  - Design: n = 1,586 families with children aged 7–17 (May–June 2020), compared with the pre-pandemic BELLA cohort (n = 1,556).
  - Mental-health problems **17.8% vs 9.9%**; low health-related quality of life 40.2% vs 15.3%; anxiety 24.1% vs 14.9%.
  - Parent-reported "abnormal" rates during the pandemic: hyperactivity 14.6%, emotional 13.3%, peer problems 11.5%, conduct 10.0%.
  - Effect sizes were small for total problems, hyperactivity and peer problems (Cohen's f² = 0.04, 0.03, 0.05) and **negligible for conduct problems (f² = 0.01)**.
  - Limitation: the online panel is compared with a different pre-pandemic survey mode. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-021-01726-5)
- **Pelotas 2004 birth cohort (Brazil).** Maruyama JM et al. *J Adolesc Health* 2024. DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.10.016.
  - Design: N = 1,949; T1 Nov 2019–Mar 2020 (mean age 15.7), T2 Aug–Dec 2021 (mean age 17.4). Self-reported SDQ.
  - Mean increases: total +1.071, **hyperactivity/inattention +0.208**, emotional +0.409, **peer problems +0.434** (all p < .001). No increase in conduct problems is reported.
  - Risk factors: harsh parenting and maternal depression. Emotion regulation was protective.
  - Limitation: no non-pandemic control, so age-related change is confounded. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.10.016)
- **Pelotas 2015 birth cohort, young children.** Murray J et al. *JAACAP* 2023. DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2022.07.832.
  - Design: 2,083 children assessed at age 4 (2019) and again in 2020, during a long school closure.
  - **No significant population-level increase in child conduct, emotional or hyperactivity problems.** Only maternal depression rose.
  - Within families, increases tracked maternal anxiety (β > 0.20 for conduct, emotional and hyperactivity problems), partner criticism (β = 0.21, conduct) and harsh parenting (β > 0.20, conduct and hyperactivity).
  - "Children's isolation was not associated with their mental health." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2022.07.832)
- **UK Household Longitudinal Study, adolescents 10–15 (UK).** Pierce M et al. *BJPsych Open* 2025. DOI 10.1192/bjo.2025.10801.
  - Design: 6,471 adolescents with any wave since 2015; 2,300 answered pandemic waves (Jul 2020, Nov 2020, Mar 2021).
  - **No change versus pre-pandemic trends** (total SDQ β = 0.05, 95% CI −0.42 to 0.51).
  - Subscales mostly followed pre-existing trends, "excepting a slight improvement in conduct problems (β = −0.26)". The CI is printed as "0.12 to 0.40", which is internally inconsistent in the abstract. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjo.2025.10801)
- **Same UK panel, earlier analysis.** Hu Y, Qian Y. *J Adolesc Health* 2021. DOI 10.1016/j.jadohealth.2021.04.005.
  - N = 886 aged 10–16; person fixed effects.
  - Adolescents with better-than-median pre-pandemic mental health showed **increases in emotional, conduct, hyperactivity and peer problems and decreases in prosocial behaviour**.
  - Those with worse-than-median baseline showed the **opposite changes**, consistent with regression to the mean. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2021.04.005)
- **UK nationally representative panel, social-capital buffering.** Laurence J, Calvey B. *J Youth Adolesc* 2025. DOI 10.1007/s10964-025-02295-5.
  - n = 1,203, mean age 12.2; pre-pandemic baseline 2017–18.
  - Reports increases in internalizing and externalizing SDQ difficulties. Only perceived neighbourhood safety consistently buffered externalizing. No pooled effect size in the abstract. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-025-02295-5)
- **UK clinical cohort (South London, CYPHER-EHR linkage).** Ching BCF et al. *JCPP Advances* 2026. DOI 10.1002/jcv2.70115.
  - n = 388 service users aged 5–17.
  - Emotional and behavioural symptoms "were not significantly different from pre-pandemic to 2021". Symptoms were high and stable. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.70115)
- **COPSY trajectories.** Kaman A et al. *Child Adolesc Psychiatry Ment Health* 2024. DOI 10.1186/s13034-024-00776-2.
  - Design: 744 youths aged 7–20, five waves (May 2020–Oct 2022).
  - 64–74% stayed stably low on internalizing and externalizing problems.
  - **Externalizing problems deteriorated in 18%**; about 10% had persistently high internalizing problems; 2–18% improved. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-024-00776-2)
- **COH-FIT adolescents (multinational, 14–17).** Solmi M et al. *JAACAP* 2025. DOI 10.1016/j.jaac.2024.07.932.
  - N = 8,115.
  - WHO-5 well-being worsened by 5.55 points (**d = 0.27**); psychopathology score rose 6.74 points (**d = 0.28**).
  - WHO-5 < 50 rose from 9% to 17%. Values returned near pre-pandemic after more than 2 years.
  - Limitation: pre-pandemic values were recalled retrospectively. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2024.07.932)
- **COH-FIT children 6–13, 12 countries.** Agorastos A et al. *Eur Neuropsychopharmacol* 2026. DOI 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2025.112741.
  - N = 6,067.
  - WHO-5 **−4.59 (95% CI −6.18 to −2.99)**; psychopathology score **+6.68 (4.48–8.88)**; both "returning to near pre-pandemic levels." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2025.112741)
- **Children born during the pandemic (USA).** Gray SAO et al. *Child Development* 2026. DOI 10.1093/chidev/aacaf041.
  - N = 407 followed from about 15 months.
  - Pandemic stressors predicted higher *initial* externalizing and internalizing levels but not change over time. Social-emotional competence showed a "long tail" of slower growth.
  - Its discussion states that rates of clinically significant problems were "near or slightly below the normative, expected rates based on BITSEA norms", explicitly contrasting with Alcon et al. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacaf041); [Scite contrasting excerpt via verify_papers](https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint6040062)

### Inferences
- Effects follow a clear gradient: internalizing, well-being and loneliness are the most robust; hyperactivity/inattention and peer problems show small, fairly consistent increases; conduct problems and aggression are the least consistent (null, negligible or improved in COPSY, UKHLS and Pelotas 2015).
- The CBCL aggression signal in preschoolers (d ≈ 0.28) has a CI that crosses zero.
- Where externalizing increased, studies repeatedly link it to family-process variables: harsh parenting, maternal anxiety, partner conflict. They link it much less to measured isolation. This points to a family-stress pathway rather than a direct effect of reduced peer contact (see Q7).
- Several "increases" may partly reflect normal age-related change and pre-existing secular trends. Pelotas 2004 and Park et al. 2024 (see Q5) adjust for age differently. The UKHLS analysis, which models pre-pandemic trends, found essentially no change.

### Gaps
- Pooled effect sizes and CIs for externalizing, social-emotional skills and stringency moderation in Stacheder et al. 2026 could not be extracted: academic.oup.com was blocked and Scite had no indexed full text.
- The pooled effect size for Rogers & MacLean 2023 is not available (full text restricted).
- No meta-analysis was found that pools **irritability** or **emotion regulation** specifically against pre-pandemic baselines. Measures are heterogeneous (SDQ, CBCL, PROMIS anger, temperament scales).

## Q2. Is there a dose–response with length of school/preschool closures? Natural experiments (Sweden), cross-country comparisons, and Czech evidence

### Takeaway
There is **moderate, mostly correlational evidence** of a dose–response.
- **Supporting:** a 2026 meta-regression found that stricter restrictions predicted larger increases in externalizing problems and larger declines in well-being. A Swedish difference-in-differences study found no SDQ change in 3-year-olds who stayed in (open) preschool, but a temporary rise in children cared for at home.
- **Weakening:** a multinational survey found only minimal associations with the stringency index.
- **Opposite direction:** US natural experiments found school closures were followed by *fewer* youth suicides and less bullying.

Czechia did have one of the longest closures in Europe (about 38 weeks by April 2021; more than 200 days of full closure Jan 2020–May 2021 per OECD). However, **no Czech study with a pre-pandemic baseline measuring externalizing behaviour was found**. Czech evidence is cross-sectional or parent-perception based.

### Cited Findings

**Cross-study and cross-country dose–response**
- **Stacheder et al. 2026** meta-regression: "Increases in restriction stringency were associated with stronger declines in well-being and greater increases in externalizing problems, whereas higher restriction levels were associated with stronger increases in internalizing problems." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag090)
- **COH-FIT.**
  - Children: changes were "significantly but minimally associated to COVID-19 deaths/stringency index". School closure was a significant individual-level correlate of worse well-being and psychopathology. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2025.112741)
  - Adolescents: school closure was associated with worse WHO-5 well-being but *not* with the psychopathology score. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2024.07.932)
- **Viner et al. 2022:** closure durations in first-wave studies ranged from only 1 week to 3 months, too narrow and too confounded to estimate a dose–response. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5840)

**Natural or quasi-experiments**
- **Sweden, preschools kept open (strongest design found).** Hjern A et al. "Behavioural and Emotional Symptoms Did not Increase During the COVID-19 Pandemic in Swedish Preschool Children." *Acta Paediatrica* 2025. DOI 10.1111/apa.70410.
  - Design: Stockholm, **N = 71,917 three-year-olds**, parent SDQ at routine child-health visits 2018–2023; difference-in-differences comparing preschool attendees with children in home care.
  - Preschool attendees: means and high scores "remained the same before, during and after the pandemic."
  - Home care: total score **+6.0%** relative to attendees, and **emotional symptoms +14%**. Both returned to pre-pandemic levels when measures lifted.
  - Limitations: repeated cross-section; home care is self-selected. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.70410)
- **Sweden, compulsory schools open.** Vira EG, Skoog T. *SSM Popul Health* 2021. DOI 10.1016/j.ssmph.2021.100942.
  - N = 849 middle-school students, two waves about a year apart.
  - "No differences in emotional problems" and "mostly negligible" decreases in psychological adjustment, but significant declines in school adjustment. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssmph.2021.100942)
- **Sweden, adolescents (Stockholm).** Nyberg G et al. *Front Public Health* 2023. DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2023.1115789.
  - Design: 585 followed from autumn 2019 to spring 2021, mean age 14.9 at baseline.
  - Health-related quality of life −1.7 (−2.3 to −1.2); psychosomatic problems +1.8 (1.3 to 2.3); high stress 28% → 42%.
  - These changes were *unrelated* to changes in accelerometer-measured activity or screen time. So Sweden's open-school policy did not prevent all adolescent deterioration. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1115789)
- **US in-person schooling and youth suicide.** Hansen B, Sabia JJ, Schaller J. *J Human Resources* 2024;59(S):S227–S255 (NBER w30795).
  - Design: staggered county reopening, proxied by school foot-traffic data, in a difference-in-differences framework.
  - Youth suicides **fell 25% in March 2020** when schools closed. **Return to in-person schooling increased suicides among 12–18-year-olds by 12–18%.**
  - Bullying is suggested as the mechanism (auxiliary analyses only). — [Source (JHR)](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/59/S/S227); [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30795/w30795.pdf)
- **US bullying.** Bacher-Hicks A et al. *AER: Insights* 2022. DOI 10.1257/aeri.20210456.
  - Internet searches for school bullying and cyberbullying **dropped 30–35%** with remote learning, then partially returned toward pre-pandemic levels as in-person schooling resumed. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1257/aeri.20210456)
- **US schooling mode (cross-sectional).** Hawrilenko M et al. *JAMA Netw Open* 2021. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.24092 (erratum issued).
  - N = 2,324 parents, December 2020, parent SDQ.
  - Older children in remote schooling had more difficulties (standardized ES **0.23, 95% CI 0.07–0.39 per year of age**). Very small effects favoured remote schooling for younger children.
  - Lower-income children benefited less from in-person schooling. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.24092); [Erratum](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.32935)
  - A later study cited by Scite found "no association between virtual learning and worse indicators of poor mental health and suicide outcomes" (PLOS Mental Health). — [Scite contrasting excerpt, citing DOI 10.1371/journal.pmen.0000409](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000409)

**Czech closure duration (verification)**
- **Statista (CEE only, as of 12 Apr 2021):** "the most extended school closures have been in Czechia with 38 weeks and Slovakia with 37 weeks"; Estonia 16 weeks. — [Source](https://statista.com/statistics/1233186/cee-school-lockdown-duration)
- **OECD / joint UNESCO–UNICEF–World Bank–OECD survey (search summary):** closures in the Czech Republic exceeded **200 days between January 2020 and May 2021**, versus fewer than 50 days in Norway, New Zealand and Spain.
  - OECD averages of full closure over the same period: 55 days pre-primary, 78 primary, 92 lower secondary, >100 upper secondary.
  - The OECD page was not opened directly. — [OECD State of Global Education](https://www.oecd.org/education/the-state-of-global-education_1a23bb23-en); [EAG 2021 component](https://read.oecd-ilibrary.org/en/publications/education-at-a-glance-2021_b35a14e5-en/full-report/component-53.html)
- **INSA network (citing ECDC):** until March 2021, Czech complete closures lasted 177 days and partial closures 125 days, **302 days total**. — [Source](https://insa.network/my-country/czech-republic/)
- **Czech media headlines (secondary, not opened):** "třetí nejdelší výpadek v Evropě" (Seznam Zprávy); "České děti jsou z Evropy doma nejdéle" (CNN Prima); "V Evropě jsou zavřené nejdéle" (Echo24).
  - The ranking (1st vs 3rd) depends on cut-off date and on whether partial closures are counted. — [Seznam Zprávy](https://www.seznamzpravy.cz/clanek/skoly-z-pohledu-cisel-minimalni-zachyt-treti-nejdelsi-vypadek-v-evrope-154541); [CNN Prima](https://cnn.iprima.cz/cesko-ma-nejdele-zavrene-skoly-v-evrope-navrat-zaku-do-lavic-je-stale-nejisty-19699); [Echo24](https://echo24.cz/a/SuJ3g/skoly-nejsou-priorita-v-evrope-jsou-zavrene-nejdele-plan-otevreni-neni-jasny)
- **ČŠI:** in spring 2020, 95% of Czech kindergartens remained closed for most of the state of emergency. — [Source (ČŠI INFO bulletin, Aug 2020)](https://csi.gov.cz/Csicr/media/Prilohy/PDF_el._publikace/Informa%c4%8dn%c3%ad%20bulletin/INFO-CSI-24-srpna-2020.pdf)

**Czech-specific studies (all lack a pre-pandemic behavioural baseline)**
- **Czech HBSC lockdown survey.** Ng K et al. *Prev Med Rep* 2021. DOI 10.1016/j.pmedr.2021.101386.
  - n = 3,440 adolescents, mean age 13.5, spring 2020.
  - 19% reported economic and 32% psychosocial disruptions to family life; **79% reported positive opportunities for family interaction or learning**. Girls and older adolescents had the worst social and mental well-being. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2021.101386)
- **Furstova J et al.** *Front Psychol* 2022. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.964313.
  - Same sample (n = 3,440). Perceived stress was strongest predicted by frequent loneliness; "having someone to talk to" was protective. Cross-sectional. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.964313)
- **Czech HBSC 2018 vs 2022.** Boniel-Nissim M et al. *Cent Eur J Public Health* 2025. DOI 10.21101/cejph.a8698.
  - n = 26,450 aged 11, 13 and 15.
  - "Marked increase in both intense and problematic social media use"; the share of non-active users fell, most notably among 11-year-olds. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.21101/cejph.a8698)
  - Palacký University reports problematic social media use rising from 5% (2018) to 8% (2022). — [Source](https://zurnal.upol.cz/nc/en/news/clanek/hbsc-study-increasing-number-of-children-suffer-from-problematic-social-media-use)
- **NUDZ + ČŠI national monitoring of 9th-graders.** May–June 2023, >6,000 pupils, all regions.
  - 40% showed signs of moderate-to-severe depression and 30% anxiety; >50% had impaired well-being; girls were affected more than twice as often as boys.
  - Cross-sectional, no pre-pandemic comparison. — [Source (NUDZ press release)](https://www.nudz.cz/fileadmin/user_upload/Tiskove_zpravy/TZ-Narodni_monitoring_dusevniho_zdravi_deti-_40___vykazuje_znamky_stredni_az_tezke_deprese__30___uzkosti._Odbornici_pripravuji_preventivni_opatreni.pdf)
- **NUDZ director P. Winkler (ČTK)** attributed rises in child depression and anxiety to the pandemic and measures, especially excessive online time and reduced peer contact. This is expert opinion, not a data analysis. — [Source](https://www.ceskenoviny.cz/zpravy/2175091)
- **PAQ Research "Život během pandemie"** (parent panel, as summarized by search; exact document not opened):
  - 55% of parents said their child's well-being or happiness worsened.
  - 27% of parents reported moderate depression or anxiety symptoms between April and November 2020.
  - These are parent perceptions only. — [PAQ/Nadace ČS PDF](https://cdn.erstegroup.com/content/dam/cz/csas/www_nadacecs_cz/dokumenty/studie-analyzy/2021/vzdelavani-pandemie-paq.pdf); [PAQ W2 (web archive)](https://webarchiv.lib.cas.cz:443/wayback/20210617175016/https://idea.cerge-ei.cz/files/PAQ_Zivot_behem_pandemie_W2_v26_ekonomika_vzdelavani.pdf)
- **PAQ/Kalibro learning loss:** 5th-grade tests from Feb 2020 vs May 2021 imply about **3 months** of lost in-person learning. Academic, not behavioural. — [Source](https://cnn.iprima.cz/deti-jsou-po-pandemicke-vyuce-tri-mesice-pozadu-nejvic-v-matematice-ukazala-analyza-35027)
- **PAQ/Učitel naživo teacher survey (n = 603):** 54% of teachers said pupil motivation worsened over the pandemic year. — [Source](https://ct24.ceskatelevize.cz/domaci/3331374-distancni-vyuka-byla-pro-ucitele-casove-narocnejsi-znamenala-i-vic-stresu-ukazal)
- **ÚZIS child psychiatry, 2021:** >58,600 outpatients up to age 20, 431 beds, ">2,600 hospitalizations".
  - The ÚZIS yearbook table reportedly gives 3,201 acute hospitalizations, which conflicts with the news figure. Over 2011–2021: about +34% outpatients and +44% acute hospitalizations, indicating a pre-pandemic upward trend. — [ČTK](https://www.ceskenoviny.cz/zpravy/2364338); [ÚZIS](https://psychiatrie.uzis.cz/res/file/oborove-statistiky/detska-psychiatrie.pdf)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the Stacheder meta-regression and the Swedish preschool difference-in-differences study are the best evidence that more restriction meant worse outcomes for young children. The Swedish home-care effect was concentrated in **emotional**, not conduct, symptoms, and it reversed.
- At country level, stringency is confounded with infection burden, economic shocks and school-reopening policies. COH-FIT's "minimal" stringency association is a caution against strong dose–response claims.
- For adolescents, closure apparently had *mixed-sign* effects: less bullying and fewer suicides (US) alongside more loneliness and lower well-being. That may explain why aggregate externalizing estimates are near zero.
- Applying the stringency moderation to Czechia (long closures, ~38 weeks / >200 days) would *predict* above-average effects. This is an extrapolation; no Czech study tested it.

### Gaps
- No primary UNESCO country-level figure for Czechia was retrieved: unesco.org was blocked by the proxy. The UNESCO "Global monitoring of school closures" master dataset exists ([UNESCO file](https://www.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2022/10/Global%20monitoring%20of%20school%20closures_Full%20dataset%20-%20Master%20file%2030092021.pdf)) but was not opened.
- A search snippet attributed to "the Czech ministry" said grades 1–5 were online for 94 days and higher grades for 141 days in 2020. The source document could not be identified, so the figure is not used.
- **No Czech longitudinal study with pre-pandemic baselines on externalizing behaviour, hyperactivity or social skills was found.** Searches covered NUDZ, ČŠI, PAQ, HBSC/UP Olomouc, Charles University and Masaryk University, in Czech and English.
- No ČŠI report specifically on post-return pupil behaviour or aggression was found. No ČŠI pandemic report on preschoolers' speech or social skills was found either.
- No Czech 2018-vs-2022 HBSC figures on life satisfaction, psychosomatic complaints or loneliness were found; the national report was not located.
- No direct Sweden-vs-neighbour cross-country comparison of externalizing behaviour was found.

## Q3. Infants and toddlers born or raised during the pandemic: social-emotional, language, motor, school readiness; replication and critiques

### Takeaway
The most replicated signal is a **small-to-moderate deficit in communication/language** and possibly in personal-social skills in pandemic-born infants and toddlers.
- Meta-analysis: OR ≈ 1.7 for communication impairment; d ≈ 0.46 for language/communication.
- **No global developmental delay** in pooled analyses.
- Early dramatic claims did not hold up as stated: the Deoni preprint reported a 27–37-point cognitive drop and was never peer-reviewed as far as found.
- Motor findings (Shuffrey et al.) rest on small historical comparison groups.
- English school-readiness data fell after the pandemic and have partly recovered. A 2021 assessment reform makes direct comparison invalid.

### Cited Findings
- **Shuffrey LC et al. (2022).** "Association of Birth During the COVID-19 Pandemic With Neurodevelopmental Status at 6 Months…" *JAMA Pediatrics* 176(6):e215563. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5563.
  - Design: New York City; 255 pandemic-born infants (114 exposed to maternal SARS-CoV-2 in utero, 141 unexposed) plus a **historical cohort of only 62**; parent-completed ASQ-3 at 6 months.
  - In-utero exposure was not associated with any ASQ-3 domain.
  - Birth during the pandemic was associated with lower:
    - **gross motor (mean difference −5.63, 95% CI −8.75 to −2.51)**
    - **fine motor (−6.61; −10.00 to −3.21)**
    - **personal-social (−3.71; −6.61 to −0.82)** — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5563)
  - A later study cited by Scite found results "in contrast to the hypothesis that lower developmental scores… would be elicited". — [Scite contrasting excerpt, citing DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2024.1426409](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1426409)
- **Hessami K et al. (2022).** *JAMA Netw Open*. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.38941.
  - 8 studies, 21,419 infants (11,438 screened during the pandemic, 9,981 before).
  - Overall neurodevelopment in the first year was not changed.
  - **Communication impairment OR 1.70 (95% CI 1.37–2.11)**; no differences in gross motor, fine motor, personal-social or problem-solving.
  - Maternal infection: fine motor OR 3.46 (1.43–8.38). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.38941)
- **O'Connor H et al. (2025).** *J Dev Behav Pediatr*. DOI 10.1097/dbp.0000000000001352.
  - 10 studies, 6 countries.
  - Overall development: **d = 0.28 (95% CI −0.33 to 0.88), not significant**; developmental delay **OR 1.44 (0.77–2.67), not significant**.
  - **Language/communication d = 0.46 (p = 0.003), OR 1.72 (p = 0.01)**; personal-social-emotional d = 0.45 (p = 0.04).
  - Cognition −0.05, fine motor 0.18, gross motor 0.17 (all not significant). Heterogeneity I² 78.6–98.5%. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1097/dbp.0000000000001352)
- **Alcon et al. (2024):** ASQ-3 changes were "negligible-to-small" and CIs crossed zero, e.g. communication d = 0.274 (−0.08 to 0.628). The sign convention is described as "improvements". This conflicts with the communication signal in Hessami and O'Connor. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/psycholint6040062)
- **Byrne S et al., CORAL, Ireland, 12 months.** *Arch Dis Child* 2023. DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2021-323441.
  - Design: 309 babies born in the first lockdown vs 1,629 from a historical cohort recruited 2008–2011; parent-reported milestones.
  - One meaningful word **76.6% vs 89.3%, RR 0.86 (0.80–0.92)**.
  - Pointing **83.8% vs 92.8%, RR 0.91 (0.86–0.96)**.
  - Waving bye-bye 87.7% vs 94.4%, RR 0.94 (0.90–0.99).
  - Limitations: parent report; historical cohort a decade older. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1136/archdischild-2021-323441)
- **CORAL, 24 months.** *Arch Dis Child* 2023;108(10):846–851. Per search summary; the DOI is probably 10.1136/archdischild-2022-325271 (inferred from the PMC file name, not verified).
  - Communication below cut-off **11.9% vs 5.4%** (p < 0.01); mean communication score 49.5 vs 53.7.
  - Fine motor, problem-solving and personal-social scores were broadly similar; fewer CORAL children fell below the personal-social cut-off.
  - **CBCL behaviour: no difference after multivariable adjustment.** — [UCC record](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/developmental-and-behavioural-outcomes-at-2-years-in-babies-born/); [news summary](https://neurosciencenews.com/language-development-covid-babies-23602)
- **Sato K et al. (2023).** *JAMA Pediatrics*. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.2096.
  - Design: Japanese municipality nursery centres; teacher-rated Kinder Infant Development Scale; 447 children followed from age 1 to 3 and 440 from 3 to 5; pre-pandemic comparison cohort.
  - At age 5, pandemic-exposed cohorts were **4.39 months behind (95% credible interval −7.66 to −1.27)**. No delay at age 3 (+1.32; −0.44 to 3.01).
  - Nursery care quality was protective at age 3 (2.01; 0.58–3.44). Parental depression amplified the age-5 delay (interaction −2.62; −4.80 to −0.49). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.2096)
- **Deoni SCL et al. (medRxiv preprint).** DOI 10.1101/2021.08.10.21261846.
  - Design: 700 children assessed with the Mullen Scales, 2011–2021.
  - Claimed "average decrease of 27–37 points" in infants born since mid-2020; maternal stress was not associated.
  - PubMed still lists it as medRxiv. No peer-reviewed version was found.
  - Critiques: "backlash from the research community" over preprint media coverage (Scientific American); assessment by masked researchers questioned (EdSource). — [Preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/2021.08.10.21261846); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pandemic-generation/); [EdSource](https://edsource.org/2021/pandemic-may-have-lowered-baby-iq-study-says/661285)
- **Ontario Birth Study.** Finegold K et al. *Arch Womens Ment Health* 2026. DOI 10.1007/s00737-026-01686-2.
  - N = 862.
  - Prenatal pandemic exposure was associated with lower gross motor skills at 24 months. This link was only *marginally* mediated by maternal prenatal internalizing symptoms. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-026-01686-2)
- **England school readiness: EYFSP "Good Level of Development" (GLD).**
  - 2018/19: **about 72%**. — [Children's Commissioner](https://www.childrenscommissioner.gov.uk/2022/11/25/latest-early-years-foundation-stage-results/)
  - 2021/22: **65% (65.2%)**, the first collection since 2018/19 because 2019/20 and 2020/21 were cancelled. — [DfE 2021/22](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/early-years-foundation-stage-profile-results/2021-22)
  - The profile was significantly revised in Sept 2021, so results are **"not possible to directly compare"** with earlier years. — [DfE 2023/24](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/early-years-foundation-stage-profile-results/2023-24)
  - Later years: +3 percentage points in 2022/23, +1 pp in 2023/24 (67.7%), 68.3% in 2024/25. **72.3% in 2025/26** per a secondary source. — [Twinkl news](https://www.twinkl.com/news/eyfs-results-show-biggest-annual-rise-since-2021-but-disadvantage-gap-widens); [Nesta](https://www.nesta.org.uk/data-visualisation-and-interactive/modest-increase-in-levels-of-children-reaching-a-good-level-of-development-but-attainment-gaps-persist/)

### Inferences
- The language/communication signal recurs across designs (two meta-analyses, CORAL at 12 and 24 months), but it rests on parent-report screening tools and historical controls. Secular drift and differences in reporting mode are plausible partial explanations.
- Behaviour problems in toddlers (CORAL CBCL; Gray et al. 2026 BITSEA) were **not** clearly elevated. This contradicts a simple "pandemic toddlers are more aggressive or dysregulated" narrative.
- Sato's finding (delay at age 5 but not age 3; nursery quality protective) fits a mechanism in which loss of high-quality group care and social input matters. This parallels the Swedish preschool difference-in-differences result in Q2.

### Gaps
- The full text of CORAL at 24 months and its exact DOI were not verified directly.
- No peer-reviewed publication of the Deoni RESONANCE findings was located.
- No large registry-based developmental data for pandemic-born cohorts in Czechia, or other countries with standardized direct assessment (rather than parent report), were found.
- The 2025/26 EYFSP figure (72.3%) comes from a secondary source only.

## Q4. Adolescent social deprivation: theory, and neuroimaging reports of accelerated brain maturation (Gotlib 2022; Corrigan 2024)

### Takeaway
The social-deprivation hypothesis (Orben, Tomova & Blakemore 2020) is a **theoretical viewpoint**, largely built on animal and pre-pandemic human data. Loneliness data support its premise: German adolescent loneliness rose from roughly 10–15% to 34.6% in 2020. The two prominent neuroimaging reports of "accelerated brain maturation" are **small, not designed as pandemic studies, cannot link brain changes to individual isolation, and have been formally critiqued**. They should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

### Cited Findings
- **Orben A, Tomova L, Blakemore S-J (2020).** "The effects of social deprivation on adolescent development and mental health." *Lancet Child Adolesc Health*. DOI 10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30186-3.
  - Interdisciplinary Viewpoint. Adolescence (10–24 years) is characterised by heightened sensitivity to social stimuli and peer interaction.
  - Animal research shows social isolation has unique effects in adolescence.
  - The authors caution that "the decrease in adolescent face-to-face contact might be less detrimental due to widespread access to digital forms of social interaction." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30186-3)
- **Loneliness, Germany (BELLA + COPSY).** Zoellner F et al. *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2025. DOI 10.1007/s00787-025-02779-6.
  - Ages 11–17. Loneliness was stable at **9.5–15.5% in 2003–2017**, spiked to **34.6% in 2020**, and was **20.5% in 2024**.
  - Risk factors include screen time and parental mental illness. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-025-02779-6)
- **Gotlib IH et al. (2022).** "Effects of the COVID-19 Pandemic on Mental Health and Brain Maturation in Adolescents: Implications for Analyzing Longitudinal Data." *Biol Psychiatry Glob Open Sci* 3(4):912–918. DOI 10.1016/j.bpsgos.2022.11.002.
  - Design: **matched cross-sectional comparison** of youths assessed before the pandemic (**n = 81**) and after shutdowns (**n = 82**), drawn from an ongoing longitudinal study.
  - Post-shutdown youths had more severe internalizing problems, reduced cortical thickness, larger hippocampal and amygdala volumes, and more advanced "brain age". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2022.11.002)
  - A subsequent *longitudinal* study reported "no pandemic-related effects… in the amygdala which is inconsistent with the cross-sectional findings." — [Scite contrasting excerpt, OSF preprint](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/t74uk)
- **Corrigan NM, Rokem A, Kuhl PK (2024).** "COVID-19 lockdown effects on adolescent brain structure suggest accelerated maturation that is more pronounced in females than in males." *PNAS* 121(38):e2403200121. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2403200121.
  - Design: a normative model of cortical-thickness change was built from pre-COVID scans; post-COVID scans were compared against it.
  - Mean acceleration **4.2 years in females, 1.4 years in males**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2403200121)
  - Sample size per commentary: **160 adolescents scanned pre-COVID, 130 of them post-lockdown**. — [Science Media Centre Spain (via search)](https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/pandemic-related-isolation-accelerated-brain-maturation-adolescents-especially-girls)
  - Critiques:
    - no measurement of individual isolation or of later psychological symptoms (SMC Spain);
    - pre- and post-pandemic data came from different subsets, with model-based estimates rather than within-person change, and no evidence that isolation per se caused the changes (HealthDay);
    - cortical thinning "not necessarily an indication of a problem" (R. Dahl, quoted in coverage). — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/mental-health/pandemic-isolation-may-have-caused-rapid-brain-aging-in-teen-girls)
  - Formal letter: Fine C et al. "Uncertainty of adolescent brain maturation sex difference claims." *PNAS* 2024;121(49):e2420724121. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2420724121. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2420724121)
  - Authors' reply: "Significant sex differences in accelerated cortical thinning associated with the COVID-19 lockdowns," *PNAS* 2024. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2421468121 (content not retrieved). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2421468121)

### Inferences
- Neither imaging study can separate lockdown-specific social deprivation from other pandemic stressors such as family stress, sleep shifts, screen time or infection. Neither study links brain measures to individual behaviour.
- "Accelerated maturation" is an interpretation of thinner cortex relative to a normative curve. Its functional meaning for self-regulation or aggression is unknown.
- The loneliness spike and its partial persistence (20.5% in 2024) are better-grounded evidence of social deprivation than the imaging data. They concern subjective well-being, however, not externalizing behaviour.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Corrigan 2024, the Fine et al. letter and the authors' reply were not accessible (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov was blocked; Scite restricted). Exact N, scan intervals and the authors' stated limitations come from secondary commentary.
- No replication of Corrigan's sex-difference finding in an independent cohort was found.
- No large-sample imaging (e.g., ABCD) re-analysis using a proper pandemic-timing design was retrieved in this task.

## Q5. Persistence vs recovery: follow-up through 2023–2026

### Takeaway
Recovery is **partial and outcome-specific**.
- Population symptom scores in Germany, the Netherlands and multinational COH-FIT samples improved from 2021–22 peaks but remained above pre-pandemic levels into 2023–2024, especially for internalizing problems and loneliness.
- Parent-reported **externalizing problems in the Netherlands were not elevated post-pandemic**.
- Service and treatment data (ADHD medication, psychiatric diagnoses) show sustained increases. These may reflect demand, diagnosis and access trends as much as true incidence.

### Cited Findings
- **COPSY 2020–2024.** Kaman A et al. *Bundesgesundheitsblatt* 2025. DOI 10.1007/s00103-025-04045-1.
  - 2,865 families, ages 7–22, May 2020–Oct 2024.
  - Mental health "improved again in the following years, but remained at an impaired level in fall 2024 compared to pre-pandemic levels." Crisis-related worries (war, climate, economy) increased. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00103-025-04045-1)
- **COPSY wave-by-wave.** Ravens-Sieberer U et al. *Front Public Health* 2023. DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2023.1129073.
  - N = 2,471 aged 7–17.
  - Overall mental-health problems: **18% pre-pandemic → 30–31% (W1–W2) → 27% / 29% / 23% (W3–W5, autumn 2022)**.
  - Anxiety 15% → 30% → 25%; low health-related quality of life 15% → 48% → 27%; psychosomatic complaints still rising. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1129073)
- **COPSY socioeconomic gradient 2017–2024.** Reiss F et al. *BMC Public Health* 2026. DOI 10.1186/s12889-026-26577-0.
  - Prevalence pre-pandemic 6.2% vs 14.6%, post-pandemic (2023–24) 11.0% vs 18.3%, with low-SES children more affected.
  - "Post-pandemic recovery effects are only visible for young people in higher socioeconomic status." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-26577-0)
- **Netherlands 2018–2023.** van Oers et al. *JCPP Advances* 2023. DOI 10.1002/jcv2.12213.
  - Design: two general-population samples (n = 818–1,056 per wave) and one clinical sample (n = 320–370), ages 8–18; post-pandemic waves Nov–Dec 2022 and Mar–Apr 2023.
  - General population: "parents reported **no changes in externalizing problems** but did report higher internalizing problems post-pandemic than pre-pandemic". Child-reported increases were largest for anxiety and depression and **least for anger**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.12213)
- **Canada (Calgary), into 2023.** Park S et al. *Psychol Med* 2024. DOI 10.1017/S0033291724001491.
  - Design: 1,399 children aged 9–11 at first wave; three pandemic waves (2020–Jan 2022) and one post-pandemic wave (Jan–Jul 2023); mother-reported pre-pandemic data (2017–2019).
  - After adjusting for age, anxiety, depression, **hyperactivity and inattention** followed a curvilinear course: an initial decline, then a rising trend post-COVID. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291724001491)
- **England MHCYP survey (official statistics).**
  - Probable mental disorder among 8–16-year-olds: **12.5% (2017) → 17.1% (2020) → 17.7% (2021) → 19.0% (2022) → 20.3% (2023)**, stable 2022–2023. 2,370 re-contacted participants.
  - Caveat: 2017 used a different, more detailed diagnostic assessment. — [NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2023/11/one-in-five-children-and-young-people-had-a-probable-mental-disorder-in-2023/); [NHS Digital report mirror](https://nhsd-proxy.openprescribing.net/data-and-information/publications/statistical/mental-health-of-children-and-young-people-in-england/2023-wave-4-follow-up/part-1-mental-health)
- **US NCES School Pulse Panel (experimental statistics; school-leader perceptions).**
  - **83%** of public-school leaders (2022) and **80%** (May 2024) agreed that the pandemic negatively affected students' behavioural development.
  - 56% reported more classroom disruptions from misconduct (2022). In 2023–24, 26% said lack of focus or inattention had a "severe negative impact" on learning. — [NCES press release](https://nces.ed.gov/learn/press-release/about-one-quarter-public-schools-reported-lack-focus-or-inattention-students-had-severe-negative); [THE Journal 2022](https://thejournal.com/articles/2022/07/07/majority-of-schools-say-pandemic-impacts-on-students-staffing-worse-in-most-recent-school-year.aspx)
- **US primary care, interrupted time series 2017–2023.** Lowenstein C et al. *Pediatrics* 2026. DOI 10.1542/peds.2025-072305.
  - Visit rates with anxiety diagnoses rose 71% (females 12–17) and 62% (males 12–17). Mood and ADHD diagnoses among older females remained 35% and 8.5% higher a year after the pandemic.
  - "Signs of reversion to prepandemic levels." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2025-072305)
- **US TriNetX, 13.3 million youths aged 3–18, 2018–2022.** Baweja R et al. *JAACAP Open* 2026. DOI 10.1016/j.jaacop.2026.04.006.
  - Versus pre-pandemic: diagnoses **+24%**, psychotropic medication **+35%**; ADHD medication in preschoolers **+147%**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaacop.2026.04.006)
- **Europe, ADHD medication sales.** Gimbach S et al. *BMC Psychiatry* 2024. DOI 10.1186/s12888-024-05505-9.
  - Sales dropped in 2020, returned to the predicted level in 2021, and **exceeded predicted levels by 16.4% in 2022**. Growth accelerated in 26 of 28 countries. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-024-05505-9)
- **Greater Manchester, ADHD incidence 2019–2023.** Hussey et al. *JCPP Advances* 2025. DOI 10.1002/jcv2.70042.
  - Incidence and prescribing increased throughout. Rises were much larger in females, and post-pandemic incidence in Asian females was 7× the pre-pandemic level. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.70042)
- **Germany, inpatient child and adolescent psychiatry (2019 vs 2021 half-years).** Kölch M et al. *Z Kinder Jugendpsychiatr Psychother* 2023. DOI 10.1024/1422-4917/a000935.
  - Increase in internalizing diagnoses and **decrease in externalizing problems among admitted inpatients**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1024/1422-4917/a000935)
- **COH-FIT:** well-being and psychopathology returned to "near pre-pandemic values after >2 years". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2024.07.932)
- **Stacheder 2026:** no further declines between multiple during-pandemic time points. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag090)
- **Early childhood persistence:** CORAL's communication gap was still present at 24 months — [UCC](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/developmental-and-behavioural-outcomes-at-2-years-in-babies-born/). The Japanese age-5 delay appeared later than age 3 — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.2096). The Swedish home-care SDQ effect reversed after measures lifted — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.70410).

### Inferences
- Internalizing problems and loneliness show the most persistence (COPSY 2024, NL 2023, England 2023). Externalizing shows the least (NL 2023: no change; German inpatient externalizing fell).
- Rises in ADHD diagnosis and medication after 2021 overlap with pre-existing upward trends and with service catch-up. They are weak evidence of a lasting lockdown effect on hyperactivity itself.
- School leaders' perceptions of worse behaviour (US 80–83%) contrast with parent-report cohort data showing little externalizing change. Possible reasons: classroom-context effects, such as reduced school readiness and lost routines, that parent questionnaires do not capture; or reporting biases. This is a real evidentiary tension.

### Gaps
- Few cohorts with pre-pandemic baselines report SDQ **conduct or hyperactivity subscales** at 2024–2026 follow-ups. COPSY's 2024 publications report composite measures.
- No post-2023 follow-up of CORAL or Shuffrey-type infant cohorts beyond 24 months was retrieved.
- No Czech data on persistence of behaviour problems were found.

## Q6. Screen time: how much it rose, whether it returned to baseline, and whether studies separate social deprivation from screen use

### Takeaway
- Children's screen time rose by about **84 min/day (+52%)** from a 162 min/day baseline during the pandemic, with larger rises in adolescents.
- Television and gaming largely returned toward baseline by 2022 in some cohorts. **Social media use kept rising**, though in a cohort that was also ageing.
- Screen use is associated within-person with conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention and anger in young children (small β).
- **No study found here formally decomposes the effects of social deprivation vs screen use** on externalizing outcomes. Some studies found pandemic mental-health changes unrelated to screen-time changes.

### Cited Findings
- **Madigan S et al. (2022).** "Assessment of Changes in Child and Adolescent Screen Time During the COVID-19 Pandemic." *JAMA Pediatrics*. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4116.
  - 46 studies, 146 effect sizes, 29,017 children (mean age 9).
  - Baseline **162 min/day; increase 84 min/day (1.4 h/day), i.e. +52%**. Ages 12–18: +110 min/day.
  - Larger increases in retrospective studies (116 min) than in longitudinal ones (65 min). Recreational +84 min; total including educational +68 min. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4116)
- **ABCD Study (USA).** Kiss O et al. *Health Psychol* 2023. DOI 10.1037/hea0001251.
  - n = 5,027 aged 10–13.
  - Screen time "steeply increased and remained high across all pandemic time points" (to March 2021).
  - Time in bed rose in May–Aug 2020, then fell below pre-pandemic levels from October 2020. More social media and gaming were associated with shorter time in bed and later bedtimes. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0001251)
- **South Australia census cohort.** Zhou M, Maher C et al. *JAMA Netw Open* 2025. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.43637.
  - 14,350 youths followed from Year 6 (2019) to Year 9 (2022).
  - TV, chores and electronic games "showed meaningful increases but **returned to prepandemic levels in 2022**".
  - Everyday social media use rose from **26.0% (2019) to 85.4% (2022)**. Music, art, reading, sport and "hanging out with friends" declined and stayed lower.
  - Major confound: the cohort aged from about 11.7 to 14.7 years with no comparison cohort. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2025.43637)
- **British Columbia.** Mâsse LC et al. *BMC Public Health* 2026. DOI 10.1186/s12889-025-26112-7.
  - 689 adolescents across the transition to secondary school.
  - The pandemic cohort showed decreases in screen use "but remained above pre-pandemic levels". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-26112-7)
- **TARGet Kids! (Toronto).** Lien A et al. *Front Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2024. DOI 10.3389/frcha.2024.1293404.
  - 700 children aged 0–11; mean change in screen time before vs during the pandemic **+51.1 min/day**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/frcha.2024.1293404)
- **TARGet Kids! (Toronto), April–July 2020.** Li X et al. *Can J Public Health* 2021. DOI 10.17269/s41997-021-00549-w.
  - 265 children, mean age 5.5.
  - Adherence to public-health measures was associated with **+11.3 min screen time (3.88–18.79)** and −17.2 min outdoor time (−22.07 to −12.40). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.17269/s41997-021-00549-w)
- **Czech HBSC:** problematic social media use 5% (2018) → 8% (2022) — [UPOL](https://zurnal.upol.cz/nc/en/news/clanek/hbsc-study-increasing-number-of-children-suffer-from-problematic-social-media-use). Intense and problematic use increased; non-active users declined — [Source](https://doi.org/10.21101/cejph.a8698).
- **Ontario, 4 cohorts.** Li X et al. "Screen Use and Mental Health Symptoms in Canadian Children and Youth During the COVID-19 Pandemic." *JAMA Netw Open* 2021;4(12):e2140875. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.40875.
  - Design: 2,026 children aged 2–18, 6,648 observations, May 2020–Apr 2021; two community and two clinically referred cohorts.
  - Younger children: higher TV/digital time was associated with **conduct problems (ages 2–4: β 0.22, 95% CI 0.10–0.35; age ≥4: β 0.07, 0.02–0.11)** and **hyperactivity/inattention (β 0.07, 0.006–0.14)**.
  - Older children: video games were associated with depression, **irritability**, inattention and hyperactivity.
  - Observational. More than half of the sample had prior mental-health diagnoses. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.40875)
- **Canada, preschoolers.** Fitzpatrick C et al. *Pediatr Res* 2023. DOI 10.1038/s41390-023-02485-6.
  - n = 315; cross-lagged panel model.
  - Screen time at 3.5 years predicted **anger/frustration at 4.5 (β = 0.14)**; anger/frustration did *not* predict later screen time. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-023-02485-6)
- **France, EpiCov (preprint).** Descarpentry A et al. 2023. DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-2538083/v1.
  - N = 1,089 aged 3–14; persistent high screen time over one year.
  - Peer problems **OR 1.42 (1.04–1.95)**; externalizing **OR 1.63 (1.01–2.63)** and conduct problems **OR 1.91 (1.15–3.22)** only at ages 11–14; internalizing OR 1.20 (0.90–1.59, not significant). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-2538083/v1)
- **Sweden (Nyberg 2023):** the deterioration in quality of life, psychosomatic problems and stress was "unrelated to changes in PA, sedentary time, screen time, or participation in organized sports." — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1115789)
- **Virtual vs in-person learning, Toronto.** Prajapati M et al. *Prev Med Rep* 2025. DOI 10.1016/j.pmedr.2025.103071.
  - 367 children aged 4–13.
  - Virtual learning was associated with **+0.22 h/day screen time (0.03–0.40)** but also +0.71 h outdoor time and +0.64 h physical activity, plus later sleep onset. This complicates the "closure = sedentary screens" assumption. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2025.103071)
- **COPSY:** high media consumption was "associated with stressful experiences" (2020–2024) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00103-025-04045-1). Screen time was a risk factor for loneliness in panel regressions — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-025-02779-6).

### Inferences
- Screen time is plausibly a **partial mediator** of small externalizing and inattention changes in young children (Li 2021; Fitzpatrick 2023). However, the same lockdown exposure raised screen time *and* reduced peer contact, routines and parental availability. Without designs such as open-preschool vs home-care contrasts that also measure screens, the two pathways cannot be separated.
- The rebound of TV and gaming to baseline (Australia 2022), combined with the persistent rise in social media, suggests that any persistent screen-related risk is now mostly **social-media-specific and adolescent**. That trend also predates the pandemic and coincides with ageing in the cohorts studied.

### Gaps
- **No study was found that formally separates the effect of social deprivation from the effect of increased screen use** (e.g., mediation with both measured, or a difference-in-differences design with screen data) for externalizing outcomes.
- No post-2022 Czech screen-time-by-age series comparable to Madigan's estimates was found.
- The "Plamondon 2023 JAMA Pediatrics" research letter on recreational screen time had no abstract in PubMed. Its finding (screen time declined after restrictions but stayed above pre-pandemic levels) is known only through a citing paper's excerpt and was not verified directly.

## Q7. Confounders and alternative pathways (parental stress, economic loss, family conflict/violence, bereavement, physical activity, sleep); groups who did better

### Takeaway
Within-study analyses repeatedly show that child behaviour changes track **family-level stressors** more closely than isolation itself. These include parental anxiety/depression and parenting stress (pooled r up to 0.46), harsh parenting, partner conflict and economic hardship. In one cohort, measured child isolation was unrelated to outcomes. Physical activity fell by about 20%. Sleep timing shifted. Bereavement affected over 1.1 million children globally.

Several groups **did better** during closures:
- adolescents poorly connected to school;
- some high-symptom adolescents (partly regression to the mean);
- victims of bullying, as bullying fell 30–35%;
- in some settings, adolescents' depression and anxiety (China).

### Cited Findings

**Family stress pathways**
- **Stracke M et al. (2023).** "Mental Health Is a Family Affair." *IJERPH* 20(5):4485. DOI 10.3390/ijerph20054485.
  - 83 articles, >80,000 families, 38 meta-analyses.
  - 25 showed significant parent–child mental-health associations, **r = 0.19 to 0.46**. "The largest effects were observed for the associations of parenting stress and child mental health outcomes."
  - Concurrent associations only, not causal. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20054485)
- **Pelotas 2015 (age 4–5).** Increases in child problems were associated with:
  - maternal anxiety (β > 0.20 for conduct, emotional and hyperactivity problems);
  - partner criticism (β = 0.21, conduct);
  - harsh parenting (β > 0.20, conduct and hyperactivity);
  - poverty-linked food shortages and financial problems.
  - Child isolation was *not* associated. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2022.07.832)
- **Pelotas 2004 (adolescents):** harsh parenting and maternal depression predicted increases in SDQ difficulties; higher emotion regulation was protective. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2023.10.016)
- **ECHO (USA).** Blackwell CK et al. *Pediatrics* 2022. DOI 10.1542/peds.2021-054754.
  - Pandemic-related family hardships → caregiver and youth stress (b = 0.04–0.21) → lower youth life satisfaction (b = −0.36 to −0.38).
  - Family engagement and social connectedness were protective. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2021-054754)
- **Japan:** parental depression amplified pandemic-associated developmental delay at age 5 (interaction −2.62; −4.80 to −0.49). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.2096)
- **COPSY:** low parental education and parental mental-health problems were associated with poorer child mental health; personal, family and social resources were protective. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00103-025-04045-1)

**Family violence, bereavement, physical activity, sleep**
- **Family violence.** Rajkumar E et al. *J Child Adolesc Ment Health* 2023. DOI 10.2989/17280583.2023.2261992.
  - 7 studies, Jan 2020–Jan 2021.
  - Family violence escalated children's mental-health problems, "including aggressive and self-harming behaviours". Parents with higher stress were more likely to maltreat. Small review of early studies. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.2989/17280583.2023.2261992)
- **Detection gap:** child-protection referrals were lower than expected during closures (3 studies). Maltreatment is therefore likely under-detected in administrative data. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5840)
- **Bereavement.** Hillis SD et al. *Lancet* 2021. DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(21)01253-8.
  - Modelling estimate, March 2020–April 2021: **1,134,000 children (95% credible interval 884,000–1,185,000)** lost a primary caregiver; **1,562,000** lost a primary or secondary caregiver. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(21)01253-8)
- **Physical activity.** Neville RD et al. *JAMA Pediatrics* 2022. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.2313.
  - 22 studies, 14,216 participants.
  - Total physical activity **−20% (90% CI −34% to −4%)**; higher-intensity activity −32% (−44% to −16%), about **−17 min/day of moderate-to-vigorous activity**. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.2313)
  - COPSY six-wave analysis: higher activity was dose-responsively associated with better quality of life and "small reductions in mental health problems". — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1776510)
- **Sleep:**
  - ABCD time in bed rose in early lockdown, then fell below baseline. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0001251)
  - Sweden: increased weekday sleep was associated with less anxiety (β −0.71; −1.36 to −0.06) and better quality of life (β 1.00; 0.51 to 1.49). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1115789)
  - Among youth with ADHD, sleep deteriorated more than in controls. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105502)
- **Socioeconomic inequality:**
  - Low-SES children had higher prevalence throughout, and post-pandemic recovery was seen only in high-SES groups (COPSY). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-26577-0)
  - Lower-income US children benefited less from in-person schooling. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.24092)

**Groups who did better or showed null or positive change**
- **Bullying** searches fell 30–35% during remote learning — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1257/aeri.20210456). US youth suicides fell 25% at closure and rose 12–18% with reopening — [Source](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/59/S/S227).
- **Hunan, China.** Yang Y et al. *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2023. DOI 10.1007/s00787-023-02263-z.
  - 1,534 adolescents, six waves.
  - Depressive and anxiety symptoms **decreased** during the 2020 lockdown: depression **d = −0.25**, anxiety **d = −0.30**. They stayed lower after easing. The authors speculate the cause was relief from bullying and achievement pressure. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-023-02263-z)
- **South-west England.** Widnall E et al. *IJERPH* 2022. DOI 10.3390/ijerph19116768.
  - n = 603 aged 13–14.
  - Anxiety **decreased** from pre-pandemic (Oct 2019) to lockdown (May 2020) and rose again on return to school. The largest decreases were in students least connected to school. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19116768)
- **Rotterdam at-risk adolescents.** Bouter DC et al. *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2022. DOI 10.1007/s00787-021-01935-y.
  - n = 1,022, oversampled for risk.
  - Mean scores on all six outcomes, **including oppositional defiant problems**, decreased significantly among those with high pre-pandemic severity. Regression to the mean is a likely contributor. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-021-01935-y)
- **UKHLS:** slight improvement in conduct problems versus pre-pandemic trends — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjo.2025.10801). Adolescents with worse-than-median baseline improved — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2021.04.005).
- **South London clinical cohort:** a diagnosis of intellectual disability was associated with a relative *decrease* in emotional symptoms from pre-pandemic to 2020 (−0.94; −1.60 to −0.28) and 2021 (−2.02; −2.46 to −1.58). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.70115)
- **COPSY trajectories:** 2–18% of youths improved during the pandemic. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-024-00776-2)
- **Czech HBSC lockdown survey:** 79% of adolescents reported positive opportunities for family interaction or learning new things. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2021.101386)

### Inferences
- The best-supported causal chain for **externalizing** change runs: lockdown/economic stress → parental distress, harsh parenting and partner conflict → child conduct and hyperactivity problems. Several studies found that measured child isolation adds little once family stress is accounted for (Pelotas 2015).
- Adolescent "relief" effects (less bullying and academic pressure) are real but concentrated in subgroups. They help explain null average effects and argue against treating school closure as uniformly harmful *behaviourally*. School closure still cost learning, routines and access to protection.
- Confounding by pre-existing secular trends (rising adolescent internalizing, ADHD diagnosis and social media use before 2020) is a pervasive threat to before–after comparisons.

### Gaps
- No study was found that jointly models social deprivation, screen time, sleep, physical activity and family stress as competing mediators of externalizing change.
- No Czech data on domestic violence affecting children during the closures were retrieved.
- Evidence on anxious or school-avoidant children improving comes from small or single-site samples (Widnall n = 603; Bouter n = 445/333 responders).

## Q8. How strong is the causal evidence? (cross-cutting appraisal)

### Takeaway
Causal evidence that **lockdowns or school closures per se** increased children's externalizing behaviour is **weak to moderate**. Evidence for the following is moderate:
- small increases in hyperactivity/inattention and peer problems;
- larger increases in internalizing symptoms and loneliness;
- communication delays in pandemic-born infants.

Most designs are uncontrolled pre/post or historical-cohort comparisons. Quasi-experimental designs are few:
- Swedish preschool difference-in-differences;
- US reopening difference-in-differences for suicide;
- bullying search trends;
- meta-regression on stringency.

These quasi-experiments point in different directions depending on outcome and age.

### Cited Findings
- Only 4 of 51 studies with pre- and within-pandemic data were rated high quality, and heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.13716)
- Effects of school closure could not be separated from wider lockdown measures in first-wave studies. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5840)
- Retrospective study designs inflate estimated screen-time change (116 vs 65 min/day longitudinal) — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4116). COH-FIT and some ADHD studies relied on retrospective pre-pandemic recall — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2024.07.932); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547231158750).
- Modelling pre-pandemic trends in the UKHLS removed apparent pandemic change (β = 0.05, −0.42 to 0.51). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjo.2025.10801)
- The strongest quasi-experimental childhood study (Sweden, N = 71,917) found no SDQ change in children who stayed in open preschools, and a transient emotional rather than conduct increase in home care. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.70410)
- Meta-regression links stringency to externalizing increases in children 0–12 — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag090). A multinational survey found only minimal associations with stringency — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2025.112741).
- Infant studies rely on parent-report screeners and historical controls (Shuffrey 62 controls; CORAL controls from 2008–2011). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2021.5563); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1136/archdischild-2021-323441)
- The neuroimaging studies are small and not designed for this question (81 vs 82 matched; 160/130), and are formally contested. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2022.11.002); [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2420724121)
- Retraction and correction check (Scite verify_papers on about 55 DOIs): no retractions or expressions of concern; Hawrilenko 2021 has an erratum. — [Erratum](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.32935)

### Inferences
- A fair summary for the report:
  - lockdowns coincided with small average increases in children's behavioural and attention problems and clearer increases in emotional problems and loneliness;
  - effects were larger with stricter restrictions and among disadvantaged or stressed families;
  - effects were transient for most children but persistent for a minority (about 18% with worsening externalizing in COPSY);
  - effects were partly offset by benefits for some (less bullying, less school stress).
- Claims of large, lasting increases in **aggression** attributable to lockdown are **not supported** by the cohort data with pre-pandemic baselines retrieved here. School-leader surveys (US) are the main source suggesting widespread behavioural deterioration, and they are perception-based.
- For Czechia, extrapolating the international dose–response to the country's very long closure is reasonable as a hypothesis. It has not been tested on Czech data.
