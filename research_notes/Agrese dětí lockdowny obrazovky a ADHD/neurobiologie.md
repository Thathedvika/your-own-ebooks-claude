## 1. Neurodevelopment of self-regulation: PFC and fronto-striatal/fronto-limbic maturation, dual-systems models and their critiques, sensitive periods

### Takeaway
Association cortex, including the prefrontal cortex, matures last and over the longest period, along a sensorimotor-to-association axis that continues into the third decade. This is robust. Behavioral data from 11 countries support the "dual-systems" pattern: sensation seeking peaks in late adolescence while self-regulation keeps rising into the mid-20s. The neural "maturational imbalance" version of the model is supported mostly by small cross-sectional fMRI studies and has been substantively criticized. Strong human evidence for sensitive periods comes mainly from extreme early deprivation (institutional rearing), not from ordinary variation in the environment.

### Cited Findings
**Maturation timeline**
- [B, foundational, small N] **Gogtay N et al. (2004)**, "Dynamic mapping of human cortical development during childhood through early adulthood." *PNAS* 101(21):8174–8179. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0402680101. Longitudinal MRI of **13 healthy children** scanned every 2 years for 8–10 years (ages 4–21), measuring gray-matter density. Higher-order association cortices matured only after lower-order somatosensory and visual cortices, and phylogenetically older areas matured earlier. Limitation: very small sample. Later large-sample work supports the general sequence (Sydnor 2021, below). — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0402680101)
- [A, review of multimodal data] **Sydnor VJ et al. (2021)**, "Neurodevelopment of the association cortices: Patterns, mechanisms, and implications for psychopathology." *Neuron* 109(18):2820–2846. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.06.016. Cortical development "progresses from lower-order, primary and unimodal cortices… to higher-order, transmodal association cortices subserving executive, socioemotional, and mentalizing functions" along a sensorimotor-to-association axis. The authors argue that "protracted plasticity within late-maturing association cortices… also confers risk for diverse developmental psychopathologies." — [Neuron](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2021.06.016)
- [A] **Bethlehem RAI et al. (2022)**, "Brain charts for the human lifespan." *Nature* 604:525–533. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04554-y. Normative charts built from **123,984 MRI scans of 101,457 participants** (115 days post-conception to 100 years). The charts provide centile references, so individual or group deviations (e.g., "accelerated" or "delayed" maturation) can be benchmarked. Specific milestone ages were not retrieved in this task. — [Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04554-y)

**Dual-systems / maturational-imbalance model**
- [B] **Galvan A et al. (2006)**, "Earlier development of the accumbens relative to orbitofrontal cortex might underlie risk-taking behavior in adolescents." *J Neurosci* 26(25):6885–6892. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1062-06.2006. Cross-sectional event-related fMRI, **N=37, ages 7–29**, reward-value paradigm. Adolescents showed "exaggerated accumbens activity, relative to prefrontal activity," compared with children and adults. Limitations: small N, cross-sectional, single task. — [J Neurosci](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1062-06.2006)
- [A, behavioral] **Steinberg L et al. (2018)**, "Around the world, adolescence is a time of heightened sensation seeking and immature self-regulation." *Dev Sci* 21(2):e12532. https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12532. Cross-sectional multi-method battery (self-report and performance), **>5,000 participants aged 10–30 in 11 countries**. Sensation seeking rose from preadolescence, **peaked at age 19**, then declined. Self-regulation rose steadily and **plateaued at 23–26**. The pattern was "largely similar across countries." Limitation: behavioral only, with no neural measures. — [Dev Sci](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12532)
- [Review, proponents] **Shulman EP et al. (2016)**, "The dual systems model: Review, reappraisal, and reaffirmation." *Dev Cogn Neurosci* 17:103–117. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2015.12.010. Reviewing evidence since 2008, the authors conclude that reward sensitivity rises from childhood, peaks in the late teens and then declines, while cognitive control rises linearly into the early 20s. They concede "occasional exceptions" and call the model "admittedly an oversimplification" that is useful "as a heuristic device." — [DCN](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2015.12.010)
- [Critique] **Pfeifer JH & Allen NB (2012)**, "Arrested development? Reconsidering dual-systems models of brain function in adolescence and disorders." *Trends Cogn Sci* 16(6):322–329. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2012.04.011. They cite "recent evidence that is inconsistent with dual-systems models" and argue for network-based models that map structure–function and brain–behavior relations over time. — [TICS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2012.04.011)
- [B, longitudinal, partial null] **Mills KL et al. (2014)**, "The developmental mismatch in structural brain maturation during adolescence." *Dev Neurosci* 36(3–4):147–160. https://doi.org/10.1159/000362328. Longitudinal structural MRI, **N=33 aged 7–30 (152 scans)**. Most individuals showed earlier structural maturation of the amygdala and/or nucleus accumbens than of the PFC. **However, in a subsample of 24, the presence of a mismatch was NOT related to retrospectively self-reported adolescent risk-taking or sensation-seeking.** — [Dev Neurosci](https://doi.org/10.1159/000362328)

**Sensitive periods for executive function and emotion regulation**
- [Hypothesis/review, D/C-based] **Larsen B & Luna B (2018)**, "Adolescence as a neurobiological critical period for the development of higher-order cognition." *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 94:179–195. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.09.005. Reviews cellular, circuit and systems PFC findings (GABA/parvalbumin, dopamine) and finds "remarkable correspondence" with mechanisms of sensory critical periods. This supports the *hypothesis* that adolescence is a critical period for PFC-dependent cognition. Much of the cellular evidence comes from animals. — [NBR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2018.09.005)
- [A, RCT, extreme exposure] **Nelson CA et al. (2007)**, "Cognitive recovery in socially deprived young children: the Bucharest Early Intervention Project." *Science* 318(5858):1937–1940. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1143921. RCT of institutionalized children randomized to foster care or continued institutional care, followed to 54 months. Foster care improved cognitive outcomes at 42 and 54 months, and gains were "most marked for the youngest children placed," which "suggest[s] a possible sensitive period." N was not in the retrieved abstract. Limitation: severe deprivation, which cannot be extrapolated to ordinary screen exposure. — [Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1143921)
- [B] **Gee DG et al. (2013)**, "Early developmental emergence of human amygdala–prefrontal connectivity after maternal deprivation." *PNAS* 110(39):15638–15643. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1307893110. Cross-sectional fMRI (fear faces), **N=89 (41 previously institutionalized, 48 comparison), ages 6.5–17.6**. Comparison children showed immature (positive) amygdala–mPFC coupling. Previously institutionalized children already showed the mature, adolescent-like negative coupling (group × age F(13,94)=3.53, p=0.013), mediated by cortisol. Anxiety was still higher in that group. Limitations: cross-sectional, modest N. One later study (Scite contrasting flag) did not find heightened amygdala reactivity after early-life stress in a block-design task. — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1307893110); [Scite verify](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0954579417001444)

### Inferences
- [D] The late, protracted maturation of association cortex is well established and is the biological basis for the claim that childhood and adolescent self-regulation is "under construction." It does not, by itself, show that any particular everyday exposure such as screens has lasting effects.
- [D] Human sensitive-period evidence for executive and emotion-regulation circuitry comes almost entirely from severe deprivation designs (BEIP, post-institutionalized adoptees). Extending this to typical media environments is an extrapolation, not a finding.
- [D] The dual-systems model is defensible as a behavioral heuristic (Steinberg 2018). The neural "imbalance" story is weaker: it rests on small cross-sectional fMRI studies, and Mills 2014 failed to link individual structural mismatch to individual behavior.

### Gaps
- I found no human study that defines a closing time-window for executive function or emotion regulation under non-extreme environmental variation.
- Bethlehem 2022 milestone ages (peak gray-matter volume and cortical thickness) were not retrieved, so no peak ages are cited.
- The BEIP sample size was not in the retrieved abstract.

## 2. Reward system and digital media: dopamine during gaming, neural responses to "likes," ABCD structural/functional findings, preschool screen use and white matter, internet gaming disorder imaging

### Takeaway
Gaming acutely engages striatal dopamine and reward circuitry, and seeing many "likes" activates the nucleus accumbens. These state-level responses are like those to other rewards and do not by themselves indicate harm. The single human PET study of gaming (Koepp 1998) has N=8 and no direct replication was found. Large ABCD analyses show statistically significant but very small associations between screen or social-media use and cortical structure (d or β ≈ 0.03–0.1), and the direction of causality is unknown. The widely cited Hutton 2020 white-matter study is cross-sectional with N=47. IGD imaging meta-analyses report fronto-striatal and cingulate differences, but they rest on small, mostly Chinese case-control samples, and their own authors describe the underlying literature as "divergent."

### Cited Findings
**Dopamine release during gaming**
- [B, N=8, not directly replicated] **Koepp MJ et al. (1998)**, "Evidence for striatal dopamine release during a video game." *Nature* 393(6682):266–268. https://doi.org/10.1038/30498. [11C]raclopride PET in adult men (MeSH: Adult, Male, Middle Aged). Raclopride binding in the striatum "was significantly reduced during the video game compared with baseline," the reduction "positively correlated with the performance level," and it "was greatest in the ventral striatum." **N=8 male volunteers**, and the task was learning to navigate a tank for a monetary incentive. This N and task description come from a citing paper (Mitsea et al. 2023, *Research, Society and Development*, quoted via Scite), not from the primary text, which was not retrieved. Egerton et al. (2009, *Neurosci Biobehav Rev*, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.05.005) report that in the original study R1 (a tracer-delivery/flow parameter) fell by ~13% in dorsal and ~14% in ventral striatum, and they discuss to what extent blood-flow changes could contribute to apparent binding decreases. That is a methodological caveat. Limitations: adults only, tiny N, monetary incentive confounded with gaming. — [Nature/PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1038/30498); [Egerton 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2009.05.005)
- [Context, null] **Wang GJ et al. (2000)**, "PET studies of the effects of aerobic exercise on human striatal dopamine release." *J Nucl Med* 41(8):1352–1356 (PMID 10945526; no DOI in record). **N=12** adults. Thirty minutes of treadmill running produced **no detectable change** in putamen D2 availability (4.22 vs 4.17, p<0.6). This shows that the raclopride displacement paradigm does not give positive results for every arousing activity. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10945526/)
- Replication search: a PubMed search for `raclopride AND (video game OR videogame OR video gaming)` returned only 3 records: Koepp 1998, Wang 2000 (exercise), and one further record not examined. **No independent PET replication of gaming-induced dopamine release was found.**
- [B] **Kühn S et al. (2011)**, "The neural basis of video gaming." *Transl Psychiatry* 1:e53. https://doi.org/10.1038/tp.2011.53. Cross-sectional VBM plus Monetary Incentive Delay fMRI, **N=154 14-year-olds** (IMAGEN). Frequent gamers had **higher** left ventral striatal gray-matter volume. The authors suggest this "could reflect altered reward processing and represent adaptive neural plasticity." Self-selection cannot be excluded. — [TP](https://doi.org/10.1038/tp.2011.53)

**Social media "likes"**
- [B] **Sherman LE et al. (2016)**, "The Power of the Like in Adolescence." *Psychol Sci* 27(7):1027–1035. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616645673. fMRI simulation of Instagram, **34 enrolled, 32 analyzed, ages 13–18**. The sample size was set by funding and scanner timing, with no power analysis (stated in the methods excerpt). Adolescents liked photos with many likes more often. Many-likes photos activated reward (NAcc), social-cognition, imitation and attention regions. Risky (vs neutral) photos reduced activation in the cognitive-control network. NAcc response did not differ by popularity for risky photos. — [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/5387999)
- [B, partial replication/extension] **Sherman LE et al. (2018)**, "Peer Influence Via Instagram: Effects on Brain and Behavior in Adolescence and Young Adulthood." *Child Dev* 89(1):37–47. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12838. **N=61, ages 13–21.** The behavioral conformity effect and the NAcc response to popular photos replicated. NAcc responsivity increased with age in high-school but not college students. The cognitive-control decrease for risky images appeared in high-school but not college students. — [Child Dev](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12838)
- [B] **Maza MT et al. (2023)**, "Association of Habitual Checking Behaviors on Social Media With Longitudinal Functional Brain Development." *JAMA Pediatr* 177(2):160–167. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4924. Three-year longitudinal fMRI cohort (Social Incentive Delay task), **N=169 (of 178), mean age 12.89 (range 11.93–14.52)**, rural North Carolina. At age 12, habitual checkers showed **lower** sensitivity to social anticipation: left amygdala/posterior insula/VS β −0.22 (95% CI −0.33 to −0.11), right amygdala β −0.19 (−0.30 to −0.08), right anterior insula β −0.23 (−0.37 to −0.09), left DLPFC β −0.29 (−0.44 to −0.14). Over time this sensitivity **increased** in habitual checkers (e.g., left amygdala/PI/VS β 0.11, 0.04–0.18; left DLPFC β 0.19, 0.05–0.25) and **decreased** in nonhabitual checkers (e.g., left amygdala/PI/VS β −0.12, −0.19 to −0.06). Limitations: checking frequency was self-reported once at wave 1; the groups already differed at baseline; observational design; one regional sample. The authors say the findings "suggest" an association and that further research "is needed." — [JAMA Pediatr](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4924)
- [B] **Su C et al. (2021)**, "Viewing personalized video clips recommended by TikTok activates default mode network and ventral tegmental area." *NeuroImage* 237:118136. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118136. Questionnaire plus fMRI in **young adults** (N not in the abstract). Personalized videos produced higher activation in DMN subregions and VTA than non-personalized ones. About 5.9% of TikTok users were estimated to have significant problematic use. — [NeuroImage](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2021.118136)

**ABCD and other structural/functional associations with screen use**
- [B, large N, tiny effects] **Paulus MP et al. (2019)**, "Screen media activity and brain structure in youth: Evidence for diverse structural correlation networks from the ABCD study." *NeuroImage* 185:140–153. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2018.10.040. Cross-sectional Group Factor Analysis, **N=4,277, ages 9–10**. Four screen-related latent factors "explained 37% of the variance between SMA and structural brain indices." This is shared variance within latent factors, not the effect of screens on the brain. Some, "but not all," factors corresponded with higher externalizing (Cohen's d **0.06–0.1**), lower crystallized intelligence (d **0.08–0.1**) and lower fluid intelligence (d **0.04–0.09**). There was no association with internalizing. The authors conclude these patterns have "mixed consequences." — [NeuroImage](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2018.10.040)
- [B, large N, tiny effects] **[Authors not retrieved] (2025)**, "Association of screen time with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms and their development: the mediating role of brain structure." *Transl Psychiatry*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03672-1. ABCD longitudinal analysis, **N=10,116 at baseline (ages 9–10), 7,880 at 2 years**. Baseline screen time predicted increases in ADHD symptoms (β=**0.032**, p=0.001) and thinner cortex in the right temporal pole (β −0.036), left superior frontal gyrus (β −0.028) and left rostral middle frontal gyrus (β −0.030), all FDR p=0.020. Total cortical volume "partially mediated" the screen–ADHD link at baseline (indirect β=**0.001**). — [Transl Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03672-1)
- [B, large N] **Nagata JM et al. (2026)**, "Social media use and early adolescent brain structure: Findings from the ABCD Study." *NeuroImage* 331:121860. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121860. Cross-sectional ABCD Year 2, **N=7,614, ages 10–13**, adjusted for demographics, SES, genetic ancestry, non-social-media screen time and scanner. More daily social-media hours were associated with **lower total cortical thickness and volume** across prefrontal, temporal, occipital and parietal regions. **Social-media "addiction" (SMAQ) was not significantly associated with brain morphology in ROI analysis.** The authors state that "future studies are required to establish the directionality." Effect sizes were not in the abstract. — [NeuroImage](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121860)
- [B, opposite-direction finding] **Chaarani B et al. (2022)**, "Association of Video Gaming With Cognitive Performance Among Children." *JAMA Netw Open* 5(10):e2235721. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.35721. ABCD cross-sectional case-control fMRI, **N=2,217, ages 9–10**: gamers at ≥21 h/week (n=679 stop-signal; n=800 n-back) vs non-gamers at 0 h/week. Gamers performed **better** on response inhibition and working memory and showed altered BOLD in precuneus, frontal and cingulate regions. Limitations: cross-sectional, extreme-group comparison, self-reported gaming time. — [JAMA Netw Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.35721)
- [B, low-tier journal] **[Authors not retrieved] (2026)**, "Screen Time Is Associated with Altered Neural Maturation in a Massive Adolescent Cohort." *Children* 13(7):969. https://doi.org/10.3390/children13070969. ABCD, two-timepoint diffusion/gray-matter microstructure in 13 regions. Screen time correlated positively with axonal signal in 6 regions and negatively with intracellular signal in 8. The authors interpret this as screen time possibly **accelerating** age-related development. N and effect sizes were not in the retrieved abstract. Treat with caution. — [Children](https://doi.org/10.3390/children13070969)

**Preschool screen use and white matter**
- [B, small, cross-sectional] **Hutton JS et al. (2020)**, "Associations Between Screen-Based Media Use and Brain White Matter Integrity in Preschool-Aged Children." *JAMA Pediatr* 174(1):e193869. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.3869. Cross-sectional DTI, **47 of 69 recruited, ages 3–5** (mean 54.3 months). Exposure was the parent-reported ScreenQ (15 items, scores 1–19). Higher ScreenQ correlated with lower FA and higher RD in tracts supporting language, executive function and emergent literacy (FWE-corrected p<.05, adjusted for age and income). ScreenQ was also negatively associated with expressive vocabulary (model R²=0.19), rapid naming (R²=0.28) and emergent literacy (R²=0.44); these R² values are for models that include age. Limitations: small sample from a single site, cross-sectional, parent-report composite measure, residual confounding by home literacy environment. A 2025 *Acta Paediatrica* study flagged as contrasting emphasized the socioeconomic homogeneity of earlier samples. No independent replication in a larger preschool DTI sample was found in this task. — [JAMA Pediatr](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.3869)
- [B, very small] **Horowitz-Kraus T & Hutton JS (2018)**, "Brain connectivity in children is increased by the time they spend reading books and decreased by the length of exposure to screen-based media." *Acta Paediatr* 107(4):685–693. https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.14176. Resting-state fMRI, **N=19, ages 8–12**, one private school, parent-reported time. Screen time was related to lower connectivity between the visual word form area and language/cognitive-control regions. The title's causal wording ("increased/decreased by") is not supported by the correlational design. — [Acta Paediatr](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.14176)

**Internet gaming disorder (IGD) neuroimaging**
- [B, meta-analysis of small case-control studies] **Yao YW et al. (2017)**, "Functional and structural neural alterations in Internet gaming disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 83:313–324. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2017.10.029. Findings: hyperactivation in anterior and posterior cingulate, caudate and posterior IFG (reward and cold-executive tasks); hypoactivation in anterior IFG (hot-executive); reduced gray matter in ACC, OFC, DLPFC and premotor cortex. Ns were not in the abstract. — [NBR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2017.10.029)
- [B] **Niu X et al. (2022)**, "Meta-analysis of structural and functional brain alterations in internet gaming disorder." *Front Psychiatry* 13:1029344. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.1029344. AES-SDM meta-analysis of **15 VBM studies (422 IGD / 354 controls)** and **30 task fMRI studies (617 / 550)**. Findings: reduced GMV in bilateral ACC/MCC, superior and inferior frontal gyrus and SMA; mixed hyper- and hypoactivation. Meta-regression with BIS-11 and IAT was reported at **uncorrected** p. The authors note that prior findings "were divergent." Average group sizes are small (~28 IGD per VBM study). — [Front Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.1029344)
- [B] **Xue R et al. (2025)**, "The hemispheric differences in prefrontal function of Internet game disorder and non-Internet game disorder: an activation likelihood estimation meta-analysis." *Cereb Cortex* 35(2). https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhae493. ALE meta-analysis of 39 studies, reporting differential prefrontal lateralization. — [Cereb Cortex](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhae493)

### Inferences
- [D] "Dopamine release during gaming" (Koepp) is real but generic: an engaging, incentivized, goal-directed task releases striatal dopamine, as many rewards do. It says nothing about habituation, tolerance or harm, and it came from 8 adult men with no independent replication found.
- [D] ABCD screen–brain associations are reliably detectable because N is huge, but their magnitude (d ≈ 0.04–0.1; β ≈ 0.03) is at the floor of practical significance. Different ABCD analyses point in different directions: thinner cortex with social media (Nagata), better inhibition and working memory in heavy gamers (Chaarani), "accelerated" microstructural maturation (Children 2026). This argues against a single "screen → brain damage" narrative.
- [D] Maza 2023 is the strongest longitudinal fMRI datum, but the baseline group difference at age 12 means the habitual and nonhabitual checkers differed before the follow-up period. Reverse causation, or a shared predisposition, is as plausible as a media effect.
- [D] IGD imaging describes a clinical extreme (diagnosed or problematic use), mostly in young adults in East Asian samples. It cannot be generalized to typical children's screen use.

### Gaps
- No PET study of dopamine markers (D2/D3, DAT) in children or adolescents in relation to habitual gaming or social media was found.
- No independent replication of Koepp 1998 was found. The primary full text, with exact percentage binding change, was not retrieved.
- No large-sample replication of Hutton 2020 (preschool DTI) was found.
- Effect sizes for Nagata 2026 were not in the abstract.

## 3. Does habitual high-stimulation, rapid-reward content change attention or reward processing (habituation, delay discounting, reduced tolerance of low-stimulation tasks)?

### Takeaway
Human **causal** evidence is limited to acute laboratory exposures in preschoolers lasting minutes. Those effects appear to come from fantastical content rather than editing pace, and the pace hypothesis failed when pace was isolated. Long-term human data are correlational, show small associations, and include notable reanalysis failures (Christakis 2004 TV → attention). The short-form-video meta-analysis shows a moderate **correlation** with poorer attention and inhibitory control (r ≈ −0.34), driven largely by addiction-scale measures and studies that are 87% correlational. The media-multitasking → distractibility claim (Ophir 2009) did not survive replication and a bias-corrected meta-analysis. Associations with delay discounting are small for internet/smartphone use. Animal "overstimulation" models (mice, rats) show lasting attention and reward changes, but their exposures are not analogous to children's media use.

### Cited Findings
**Human experimental (acute)**
- [B] **Lillard AS & Peterson J (2011)**, "The immediate impact of different types of television on young children's executive function." *Pediatrics* 128(4):644–649. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2010-1919. RCT, **N=60 four-year-olds**, 9 minutes of a fast-paced cartoon vs an educational cartoon vs drawing, followed by 4 EF tasks (delay of gratification, Tower of Hanoi, etc.). The fast-paced group performed worse. Limitation: only the immediate effect was measured, with no evidence of persistence. — [Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2010-1919)
- [B, partly replicates but reattributes the effect] **Lillard AS et al. (2015)**, "Further examination of the immediate impact of television on children's executive function." *Dev Psychol* 51(6):792–805. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039097. Three experiments: Study 1 N=160 (ages 4 and 6), Study 2 N=60, Study 3 N=80. The effect replicated for fast and fantastical shows. **Study 3 found that "only fantastical shows, regardless of their pacing, disrupted 4-year-olds' EF."** Pace per se was not the driver. — [Dev Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039097)
- [B, null for pace on inhibition] **Kostyrka-Allchorne K, Cooper NR, Simpson A (2019)**, "Disentangling the effects of video pace and story realism on children's attention and response inhibition." *Cogn Dev* (2019; volume and pages not retrieved). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2018.12.003. **N=187 four-year-olds**, with pace and realism manipulated orthogonally. "Irrespective of the pace, watching the videos which presented unrealistic stories **improved** children's response inhibition." Fast pace produced faster responding only for realistic stories. A companion ERP study (*Dev Neuropsychol* 2019, https://doi.org/10.1080/87565641.2019.1630628) "failed to provide evidence that pace had consequences for children's inhibitory behaviour," as characterized in a Scite citation statement. — [Cogn Dev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2018.12.003)
- [B] A further *Pediatrics* 2018 study, "Television Format and Children's Executive Function" (https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-2674), revisited pacing. Its introduction notes that Anderson et al. (1977, Sesame Street with artificially increased pace) and Geist & Gibson (Power Rangers vs Mister Rogers) **found no pace effect on EF**. Its own results were not retrieved (abstract access restricted).

NLSY data, **N=1,278 (age 1) and 1,345 (age 3)**. With a nonlinear specification, the association existed "if at all, only at very high levels of viewing," and "adding 2 covariates to the regression model eliminated even this modest effect." Fixed-effects estimation found "no meaningful relation." — [Child Dev](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01400.x)

- [C, independent lab, different modality] **Hadas I et al. (2016)**, "Exposure to salient, dynamic sensory stimuli during development increases distractibility in adulthood." *Sci Rep* 6:21129. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep21129. ### Inferences
- [D] The most-cited human experiment (Lillard & Peterson 2011) shows a transient effect of minutes. The same group's follow-up attributed it to fantastical content, not pace, and an independent N=187 study found no pace effect on inhibition. The specific "fast-paced editing erodes self-control" mechanism is **not established** in humans.
- [D] Habituation and "reduced tolerance for low-stimulation tasks" is a plausible theoretical mechanism (Groves & Thompson 1970, as cited by Nguyen 2025). I found **no human causal study in children** directly testing it, for example by randomizing to high- vs low-stimulation media for weeks and measuring boredom tolerance or sustained attention afterward.
- [D] The animal models show that developmental sensory overstimulation *can* alter attention and reward circuits in rodents. Their exposures (6 h/day of flashing lights at 70 dB; 1 h/day of odor switching) are far from children's media use, and the Christakis paradigm comes from one lab.
- [D] Short-form-video correlations (r ≈ −0.34) are larger than typical screen-time effects. Because they are driven by addiction-scale measures, they plausibly reflect pre-existing poor self-regulation leading to problematic use, as much as or more than use leading to poorer attention.

### Gaps
- There are no long-term randomized exposure studies in children on attention or reward outcomes. Short-term reduction RCTs exist (see Q7), but they have no neural outcomes.
- The primary-text values for the attention and inhibition subdomains and the moderator analysis in Nguyen 2025 were not verified. Only the overall cognition effect (r = −.34) was verified from full text.
- The results of the *Pediatrics* 2018 "Television Format" study were not retrieved.

## 4. Sleep as a mechanism: evening screens → sleep (light/melatonin, arousal, displacement); experimental sleep restriction → ADHD-like symptoms; circadian findings in ADHD

### Takeaway
(a) Observationally, bedtime device access and use are robustly associated with shorter and poorer sleep: 20 studies, 125,198 children, OR ≈ 1.5–2.7, though with high heterogeneity. Children's melatonin is highly light-sensitive, more so than adults'. Direct experimental effects of 1 hour of tablet light or pre-sleep gaming on adolescent sleep are small, and a family screen-reduction RCT found no change in EEG-measured sleep. (b) Experimental sleep restriction of even ~1 hour per night causally worsens attention, emotional lability, irritability and oppositionality in children and adolescents. This is consistent across several small RCTs and crossovers. Hyperactivity-impulsivity is usually **not** increased, and objective attention effects are inconsistent. (c) Delayed circadian phase (later DLMO, later sleep onset) is consistently found in ADHD subgroups with sleep-onset insomnia. Melatonin corrects the phase but did not improve ADHD behavior in the one RCT retrieved.

### Cited Findings
**Screens → sleep: observational**
- [A, but cross-sectional] **Carter B et al. (2016)**, "Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis." *JAMA Pediatr* 170(12):1202–1208. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341. **20 cross-sectional studies, 125,198 children aged 6–19 (mean 14.5).** Bedtime device use was associated with inadequate sleep quantity OR **2.17 (1.42–3.32), I²=90%**, poor sleep quality OR **1.46 (1.14–1.88), I²=76%**, and excessive daytime sleepiness OR **2.72 (1.32–5.61), I²=50%**. **Access without use** was also associated: OR 1.79 (1.39–2.31), 1.53 (1.11–2.10) and 2.27 (1.54–3.35). Limitations: all included studies cross-sectional, high heterogeneity. — [JAMA Pediatr](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)
- [A, observational] **Hale L & Guan S (2015)**, "Screen time and sleep among school-aged children and adolescents: a systematic literature review." *Sleep Med Rev* 21:50–58. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2014.07.007. **67 studies (1999–2014).** Screen time was adversely associated with sleep outcomes, mainly shorter duration and delayed timing, **in 90% of studies**. The listed limitations include "causal association not confirmed" and measurement error. — [SMR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2014.07.007)

**Light and melatonin: experimental**
- [B, adults] **Chang AM et al. (2015)**, "Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness." *PNAS* 112(4):1232–1237. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112. Controlled within-subject inpatient study in **adults** (N not in the abstract): reading on a light-emitting eReader vs a printed book in the hours before bed. The eReader led to longer sleep latency, reduced evening sleepiness, reduced melatonin, a later circadian phase and reduced next-morning alertness. Limitation: adults, an unusually long nightly exposure protocol, not children. - [B, very small analyzable n] **Higuchi S et al. At 580 lux, melatonin suppression was **88.2% in children (n=5) vs 46.3% in adults (n=6)**. Ordinary room light at home suppressed melatonin in children (n=15) but not in adults (n=11). — [JCEM](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2014-1629)

In 62% of children melatonin stayed below 50% of baseline for ≥50 minutes after the light ended. - [B] **Fallone G et al. (2005)**, "Experimental restriction of sleep opportunity in children: effects on teacher ratings." *Sleep* 28(12):1561–1567. https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/28.12.1561. - [A-ish, systematic review] **Coogan AN & McGowan NM (2017)**, "A systematic review of circadian function, chronotype and chronotherapy in ADHD." *Atten Defic Hyperact Disord* 9(3):129–147. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-016-0214-5. **62 studies, 4,462 ADHD patients.** "Consistent evidence" links ADHD with eveningness and phase delay (DLMO, sleep onset). Melatonin may help ADHD-related sleep problems. Clock-gene association studies are few. — [ADHD](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-016-0214-5)
- [Review/hypothesis, D] **Bijlenga D, Vollebregt MA, Kooij JJS, Arns M (2019)**, "The role of the circadian system in the etiology and pathophysiology of ADHD: time to redefine ADHD?" *Atten Defic Hyperact Disord* 11(1):5–19. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-018-0271-z. States that delayed sleep phase disorder is "prevalent in 73–78%" of children and adults with ADHD, and proposes that "a part of the ADHD symptoms are the result of chronic sleep disorders." The authors themselves label this a "novel view." — [ADHD](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-018-0271-z)
- [B, genetic] **Hernandez LM et al. (2022)**, "Decoupling Sleep and Brain Size in Childhood: An Investigation of Genetic Covariation in the ABCD Study." *Biol Psychiatry Glob Open Sci* 3(1):139–148. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2021.12.011. ABCD discovery N=4,428 and replication N=4,728, ages 9–10. Childhood insomnia had heritability h²=0.15 and **genetic correlations rg>0.80 with externalizing and ADHD symptoms**. ADHD polygenic scores predicted smaller surface area and later insomnia and externalizing symptoms. — [BPGOS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2021.12.011)

### Inferences
- [D] Sleep loss is the best-supported **causal** pathway by which a child's environment can produce ADHD-*like* daytime problems: inattention, irritability, oppositionality and emotional lability. The experimental profile differs from full ADHD, because hyperactivity-impulsivity is typically **not** increased (Fallone 2005; Baum 2014; Becker 2019).
- [D] The link from screens to sleep is strong correlationally, but the experimental "dose" effects of light or gaming before bed are small in adolescents (Heath, Weaver), and a 2-week family screen-reduction RCT did not change EEG sleep. Displacement of sleep time and device access in the bedroom (Carter's "access without use") may matter more than melatonin suppression per se. That is inference; the mechanisms have not been decomposed experimentally in children.
- [D] Young children's very high melatonin sensitivity (Higuchi; Crowley; Hartstein) makes evening light a biologically plausible concern for preschoolers specifically. No study retrieved shows this translates into daytime self-regulation deficits.
- [D] Circadian delay in ADHD and the genetic overlap between insomnia and ADHD (Hernandez) imply **bidirectional confounding**. Children genetically prone to ADHD may both use screens later at night and sleep later, so observational screen–sleep–ADHD chains cannot be read causally.

### Gaps
- No experimental study combining evening screen use → sleep loss → next-day self-regulation in pre-pubertal children was found.
- Chang 2015's N and the individual null RCTs cited in the 2024 Frontiers paper were not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of experimental sleep restriction in children specifically was retrieved; the 2021 BSM paper counts only 8 studies.

## 5. Stress physiology: HPA axis, chronic stress and social isolation in the developing brain; post-pandemic brain-maturation findings

### Takeaway
Chronic stress affects PFC, hippocampus and amygdala in timing-dependent ways. The mechanism is robust in animals and supported in humans, mostly by extreme-adversity studies. A leading framework holds that early adversity **accelerates** fronto-limbic maturation (stress acceleration). Two pandemic MRI studies reported "accelerated" adolescent brain maturation after lockdowns. Both are small, one is a matched cross-sectional comparison, and the sex-difference claim in Corrigan 2024 failed a direct statistical re-test (1 of 68 regions). A longitudinal preprint did not reproduce Gotlib's amygdala finding.

### Cited Findings
- [Review, A for the mechanism] **Lupien SJ, McEwen BS, Gunnar MR, Heim C (2009)**, "Effects of stress throughout the lifespan on the brain, behaviour and cognition." *Nat Rev Neurosci* 10(6):434–445. https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2639. Chronic exposure to stress hormones affects brain structures involved in cognition and mental health. Effects depend on the timing and duration of exposure and on gene × prior-adversity interactions. — [NRN](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2639)
- [Review] **Koss KJ & Gunnar MR (2018)**, "Annual Research Review: Early adversity, the HPA axis, and child psychopathology." *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 59(4):327–346. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12784. Reviews the evidence for the HPA axis as mediator between adversity and psychopathology, and flags gaps in developmental timing, sex differences, stress buffering and epigenetics. — [JCPP](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12784)
- [Hypothesis, D] **Callaghan BL & Tottenham N (2016)**, "The Stress Acceleration Hypothesis: Effects of early-life adversity on emotion circuits and behavior." *Curr Opin Behav Sci* 7:76–81. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cobeha.2015.11.018. Across species, early adversity "leads to accelerated maturation of emotion circuits." The authors interpret this as "early system adaptation." — [COBS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cobeha.2015.11.018)

This is explicitly a hypothesis. — [NRN](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-021-00457-5)
- [B] Gee et al. 2013 (see Q1): accelerated amygdala–mPFC coupling after institutional care, N=89, mediated by cortisol.
- [C] **Makinodan M et al. (2012)**, "A critical period for social experience-dependent oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination." *Science* 337(6100):1357–1360. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1220845. - [B] **Tomova L et al. - [Viewpoint] **Orben A, Tomova L, Blakemore SJ (2020)**, "The effects of social deprivation on adolescent development and mental health." *Lancet Child Adolesc Health* 4(8):634–640. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30186-3. Animal research shows adolescent-specific effects of isolation. The authors note that digital social interaction "might" make reduced face-to-face contact "less detrimental." This is a viewpoint, not data. — [Lancet CAH](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30186-3)

**Post-pandemic brain-maturation studies**
- [B] **Gotlib IH et al. (2022/2023)**, "Effects of the COVID-19 Pandemic on Mental Health and Brain Maturation in Adolescents: Implications for Analyzing Longitudinal Data." *Biol Psychiatry Glob Open Sci* 3(4):912–918. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2022.11.002. **Matched comparison of 81 youths assessed pre-pandemic vs 82 assessed after shutdowns** (ages were not in the retrieved abstract). - [B] **Corrigan NM, Rokem A, Kuhl PK (2024)**, "COVID-19 lockdown effects on adolescent brain structure suggest accelerated maturation that is more pronounced in females than in males." *PNAS* 121(38):e2403200121. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2403200121. It found accelerated thinning equivalent to **4.2 years in females and 1.4 years in males**. N was not in the abstract. — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2403200121)

They also cite limitations in "sample size and how normative brain aging was estimated." Further letters by Fine et al. and Rippon, with author replies (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2421468121; https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2426640122), were identified but their text was not retrieved. — [PNAS letter full text via Scite](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2421462122)
- [B] **Corrigan NM et al. (2026)**, "Accelerated maturation of brain circuits and executive function in adolescents who experienced the COVID-19 pandemic." *Imaging Neuroscience*. https://doi.org/10.1162/imag.a.1307. Diffusion MRI with normative models. Several white-matter pathways showed "accelerated development" post-lockdown, greater in females, and **post-lockdown EF performance also showed accelerated development**. N was not in the abstract. — [Imaging Neurosci](https://doi.org/10.1162/imag.a.1307)

### Inferences
- [D] "Accelerated maturation" in pandemic MRI studies is defined statistically as deviation from a pre-pandemic normative trajectory. It is **not** a direct measure of harm. Corrigan 2026 reports "accelerated" EF performance, which in practice means better-than-expected EF for age. This makes a simple "the pandemic damaged the brain" reading inconsistent with their own data.
- [D] Pandemic designs cannot separate stress, isolation, changed sleep, increased screen time, infection, and scanner or season effects. None of the retrieved studies isolated screen time as a mechanism.
- [D] The animal isolation literature (Makinodan) shows real critical-period effects, but the paradigm is total isolation of juvenile mice. Adolescents during lockdown kept family and digital contact.

### Gaps
- The Ns for Corrigan 2024 and 2026 were not in the abstracts. The Fine et al. and Rippon letters were not retrieved.
- No ABCD-scale longitudinal analysis of pandemic-era brain change was retrieved in this task.
- Direct measures of HPA output (cortisol) linked to screen use in children were not searched in depth. This remains a gap.

## 6. Neurobiology of ADHD for comparison, and whether brain correlates of heavy screen use resemble ADHD correlates

### Takeaway
ADHD's structural brain differences are **robust but small**. Subcortical volumes are lower by d ≈ −0.1 to −0.19 (ENIGMA, N≈3,200), and cortical surface area is lower in children (d ≈ −0.21) but not in adolescents or adults. In a population sample (ABCD, N=10,736) differences were only d ≈ −0.06 to −0.11. Delayed cortical maturation (Shaw 2007) is supported by longitudinal reviews but with inconsistencies. Dopamine findings are mixed and confounded by medication. Screen-use correlates in ABCD are of similar tiny magnitude but in different metrics: mostly cortical **thickness**, sometimes framed as **accelerated** rather than delayed maturation. ADHD genetic liability may drive both screen use and brain differences. **No study retrieved directly tested whether screen-related brain patterns match ADHD patterns.** On current evidence they are best described as not comparable.

### Cited Findings
**Structure**
- [A] **Hoogman M et al. (2017)**, "Subcortical brain volume differences in participants with ADHD in children and adults: a cross-sectional mega-analysis." *Lancet Psychiatry* 4(4):310–319. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30049-4. ENIGMA, **1,713 ADHD vs 1,529 controls, 23 sites, median age 14 (range 4–63)**. Smaller in ADHD: accumbens d=−0.15, amygdala −0.19, caudate −0.11, hippocampus −0.11, putamen −0.14, intracranial volume −0.10. No differences in pallidum or thalamus. Effects were larger in children (<15 y; e.g., accumbens −0.19, putamen −0.18) and **non-significant in adults**. There was no effect of stimulant medication, symptom scores or comorbidity. The authors interpret this as extending the maturation-delay theory, but the design is cross-sectional. — [Lancet Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30049-4)
- [A] **Hoogman M et al. (2019)**, "Brain Imaging of the Cortex in ADHD: A Coordinated Analysis of Large-Scale Clinical and Population-Based Samples." *Am J Psychiatry* 176(7):531–542. https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2019.18091033. **2,246 ADHD vs 1,934 controls, 36 centers.** In children, surface area was lower in frontal, cingulate and temporal regions (total surface area d=**−0.21**). Thickness was lower only in fusiform gyrus and temporal pole. **There were no surface-area or thickness differences in adolescents or adults.** In Generation R (N=2,707), attention problems were associated with surface area but not thickness, consistent with ADHD as a continuous trait. — [AJP](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2019.18091033)
- [A, population sample, near-null] **Bernanke J et al. (2022)**, "Structural brain measures among children with and without ADHD in the ABCD Study cohort: a cross-sectional US population-based study." *Lancet Psychiatry* 9(3):222–231. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(21)00505-8. **N=10,736 aged 9–10 (949 with ADHD).** Only **11 of 79** measures differed after FDR correction, all reductions, with **d from −0.11 to −0.06**. Simulations showed adequate power even under diagnostic misclassification. — [Lancet Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(21)00505-8)
- [A/B, landmark, partially consistent later] **Shaw P et al. (2007)**, "ADHD is characterized by a delay in cortical maturation." *PNAS* 104(49):19649–19654. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0707741104. **223 ADHD and 223 controls, 824 scans**, longitudinal cortical thickness. Median age at which 50% of cortical points reached peak thickness was **10.5 y in ADHD vs 7.5 y in controls** (p<10⁻²⁰), with the delay most prominent in prefrontal regions. One contrasting citation: an adult study found cortical-thickness reductions persisting into adulthood, against the claim that differences disappear in adolescence (https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054711434351). — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0707741104)
- [B, systematic review] **Ishii-Takahashi A et al. (2026)**, "Longitudinal MRI studies of children with ADHD: A systematic review." *Psychiatry Clin Neurosci* 80(10):789–812. https://doi.org/10.1111/pcn.70100. **32 longitudinal studies.** Findings include a delayed peak in thickness, surface area and gyrification, and differences that diminish with age in some regions. **A meta-analysis of longitudinal total-GMV change found no significant ADHD vs TD difference.** The authors note "inconsistencies across studies," "methodological challenges," and selection bias. — [PCN](https://doi.org/10.1111/pcn.70100)

**Dopamine and noradrenaline**
- [B] **Volkow ND et al. (2009)**, "Evaluating dopamine reward pathway in ADHD: clinical implications." *JAMA* 302(10):1084–1091. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.1308. PET in **53 non-medicated adults with ADHD vs 44 controls**. DAT and D2/D3 binding were lower in left nucleus accumbens, midbrain, caudate and hypothalamic region. For example, accumbens DAT was 0.71 vs 0.63 (95% CI of the difference 0.03–0.13) and accumbens D2/D3 2.85 vs 2.68 (0.06–0.30). Inattention correlated with D2/D3 in the accumbens (r=0.35) and with midbrain DAT (r=0.37). Adults only. — [JAMA](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.1308)
- [B, meta-analysis, heterogeneity] **Fusar-Poli P et al. (2012)**, "Striatal dopamine transporter alterations in ADHD: pathophysiology or adaptation to psychostimulants? A meta-analysis." *Am J Psychiatry* 169(3):264–272. https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2011.11060940. **9 studies, 169 ADHD vs 173 controls.** DAT was **14% higher** on average in ADHD, with large heterogeneity. DAT was **lower in drug-naive patients and higher in previously medicated patients**, so the finding depends on medication history. — [AJP](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2011.11060940)
- [Review] **del Campo N et al. (2011)**, "The roles of dopamine and noradrenaline in the pathophysiology and treatment of ADHD." *Biol Psychiatry* 69(12):e145–e157. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2011.02.036. Dopamine and noradrenaline modulate fronto-striato-cerebellar circuits, and ADHD medications increase catecholamines. The "precise prefrontal cortical and subcortical mechanisms… remain to be fully specified." — [Biol Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2011.02.036)
- [Consensus] **Faraone SV et al. (2021)**, "The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement: 208 Evidence-based conclusions about the disorder." *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 128:789–818. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.01.022. Included only studies with >2,000 participants, or meta-analyses of ≥5 studies or ≥2,000 participants that assessed publication bias. It produced 208 statements endorsed by 80 authors. The specific neurobiological statements were not extracted here. — [NBR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.01.022)

**Screen-use correlates for comparison (see Q2 for details)**
- [B] ABCD screen time predicts slightly thinner superior frontal and rostral middle frontal cortex (β ≈ −0.03) and slightly more ADHD symptoms (β=0.032), with a mediation effect of β=0.001 (*Transl Psychiatry* 2025, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-025-03672-1).
- [B] Social-media hours are associated with lower cortical thickness and volume across many lobes, while social-media "addiction" is not (Nagata 2026, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121860).
- [B] Heavy gamers show *better* inhibition and working memory (Chaarani 2022, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.35721).
- [B] Genetics: ADHD polygenic scores predict smaller surface area and insomnia in ABCD (Hernandez 2022, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsgos.2021.12.011).

### Inferences
- [D] **Magnitude:** screen–brain associations (d ≈ 0.04–0.1; β ≈ 0.03) and ADHD case-control differences in the same population cohort (d ≈ −0.06 to −0.11) are both tiny. Neither yields an individual-level biomarker.
- [D] **Metric and direction mismatch:** ADHD's most consistent pediatric cortical marker is reduced **surface area** with delayed maturation (Hoogman 2019; Shaw 2007). Screen and social-media correlates are mostly reduced **thickness** (Paulus; Nagata; Transl Psychiatry 2025). In adolescence cortical thinning is the normative maturational direction, and some screen-use and pandemic studies frame it as **accelerated** maturation. "Screen brain = ADHD brain" therefore has no empirical support. If anything the framing points in the opposite direction (accelerated vs delayed), but no study has tested this directly.
- [D] **Confounding:** the genetic overlap of ADHD with insomnia and externalizing (rg>0.80), and ADHD PRS predicting smaller surface area, mean that children with higher ADHD liability may both use more screens and show ADHD-related brain features. Observational screen–brain–ADHD mediation models (β=0.001) cannot exclude this.
- [D] **Dopamine:** Koepp's acute gaming-induced release (a state measure in 8 adults) and Volkow's lower DAT/D2 availability in unmedicated ADHD adults (a trait measure) are different constructs. There are no data showing that habitual media use produces ADHD-like dopamine marker profiles in children.

### Gaps
- No study directly compared the spatial or multivariate pattern of screen-associated brain differences with ADHD-associated patterns in the same cohort, for example by spatial correlation with ENIGMA maps.
- No pediatric PET dopamine data exist in relation to media use.
- The ENIGMA ADHD lifespan and normative-modeling papers after 2019 were not retrieved, apart from the 2026 systematic review.

## 7. Neuroplasticity and reversibility: do environmentally induced changes in attention, reward or sleep regulation reverse after exposure changes, and when do plasticity windows narrow?

### Takeaway
Several stress- and sleep-related changes are demonstrably reversible. Stress-induced PFC dendritic retraction in rats and stress-induced attention and fronto-parietal changes in human adults both recovered within about a month. Sleep extension of ~27 minutes per night improved children's emotional lability within a week, although after a week at 5 hours per night two recovery nights did not fully restore sustained attention. Short screen-reduction RCTs improve some behavioral outcomes: children's SDQ difficulties (d=0.53) and adults' sustained attention. They do not improve EEG sleep, and none measured the brain. Irreversibility has been shown mainly for **severe deprivation during critical periods**: post-weaning isolation in mice and late placement in BEIP. In humans the windows for association cortex are protracted into adolescence, which implies both prolonged vulnerability and prolonged capacity for recovery.

### Cited Findings
- [C] **Radley JJ et al. (2005)**, "Reversibility of apical dendritic retraction in the rat medial prefrontal cortex following repeated stress." *Exp Neurol* 196(1):199–203. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.expneurol.2005.07.008. Rats, 3 or 6 weeks of daily restraint vs 3 weeks of stress plus 3 weeks of recovery. Apical dendritic length and branching in mPFC were reduced after stress and **restored after 3 weeks of recovery**. Adult animals only. — [Exp Neurol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.expneurol.2005.07.008)
- [B, human adults] **Liston C, McEwen BS, Casey BJ (2009)**, "Psychosocial stress reversibly disrupts prefrontal processing and attentional control." *PNAS* 106(3):912–917. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0807041106. **N=20 healthy adults** after 1 month of psychosocial stress, plus a matched low-stress control group scanned twice. Stress impaired attention-shifting and fronto-parietal connectivity, and **after 1 month of reduced stress there were no significant differences from controls**. Small N, adults. — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0807041106)
- [C, irreversible within critical period] Makinodan et al. 2012 (Q5): post-weaning isolation effects on PFC myelination **did not recover** after re-socialization, and the effect occurred only in that window.
- [A, RCT] Nelson et al. 2007 (Q1): recovery with foster care was greater for younger placement, suggesting a sensitive period. **Sheridan MA et al. (2012)**, "Variation in neural development as a result of exposure to institutionalization early in childhood." *PNAS* 109(32):12927–12932. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1200041109. MRI and EEG, **never-institutionalized n=20, institutional care n=29, foster care n=25**. Cortical **white matter** in the foster-care group did not differ from never-institutionalized children but was smaller in children who stayed in institutions. Cortical gray matter was smaller in all children with institutional history. The authors interpret this as "potential for developmental 'catch up' in white matter growth." — [PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1200041109)
- [B, RCT] Gruber et al. 2012 (Q4): a cumulative +27.36 minutes of sleep extension improved teacher-rated emotional lability and restless-impulsive behavior within the study week.
- [B, RCT] Lo et al. 2016 (Q4): after 7 nights of 5 hours in bed, **sleepiness and sustained attention had not recovered after 2 nights of 9-hour recovery sleep**. Recovery from chronic restriction is not immediate.
- [B, RCT] Van der Heijden et al. 2007 (Q4): melatonin advanced the circadian phase by ~44 minutes but **did not change ADHD behavior or cognition**. Correcting circadian timing alone did not reverse ADHD symptoms.
- [B, RCT, secondary analysis] **Schmidt-Persson J et al. (2024)**, "Screen Media Use and Mental Health of Children and Adolescents: A Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Clinical Trial." *JAMA Netw Open* 7(7):e2419881. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.19881. SCREENS trial, **89 families, 181 children (mean ages 8.6 and 9.5)**, 2 weeks at ≤3 h/week of leisure screens. SDQ total difficulties improved by **−1.67 (95% CI −2.68 to −0.67; Cohen's d 0.53)**, internalizing by −1.03 (−1.76 to −0.29), and prosocial behavior by +0.84 (0.39–1.30). Limitations: parent-reported, unblinded, 2 weeks only, a prespecified *secondary* outcome. The primary outcome paper (Pedersen 2022) found no EEG sleep change. — [JAMA Netw Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.19881)
- [B, RCT, adults] **Castelo N et al. (2025)**, "Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being." *PNAS Nexus* 4(2):pgaf017. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf017. One-month RCT in adults, with mobile internet blocked for 2 weeks and compliance tracked objectively. The intervention improved objectively measured sustained attention, mental health and well-being, and "91% of participants improved on at least one" outcome. Effects were partly mediated by more in-person socializing, exercise and time in nature. N and effect sizes were not retrieved (full text restricted). Adults, not children. — [PNAS Nexus](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf017)
- [B, meta-analysis, behavioral reversibility in IGD] **Reangsing C et al. (2025)**, "Effects of CBT on addictive symptoms in individuals with internet gaming disorders: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Psychiatry Res* 348:116425. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2025.116425. **13 studies, 1,115 participants (mean age 19.1).** CBT reduced IGD symptoms vs controls, **g=0.72 (95% CI 0.42–1.01)**. Quasi-experimental designs were included, and no neural outcomes were measured. — [Psychiatry Res](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2025.116425)
- [B, adult experience-dependent plasticity] **Kühn S et al. (2014)**, "Playing Super Mario induces structural brain plasticity: gray matter changes resulting from training with a commercial video game." *Mol Psychiatry* 19(2):265–271. https://doi.org/10.1038/mp.2013.120. Adult training study, 2 months at ≥30 min/day vs controls. Gray matter **increased** in right hippocampus, right DLPFC and bilateral cerebellum. This shows that gaming-related structural change can be in the "gain" direction and that adult brains remain plastic. N was not in the abstract. — [Mol Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1038/mp.2013.120)
- [Review] Larsen & Luna 2018 and Sydnor et al. 2021 (Q1): association cortex has protracted plasticity extending through adolescence. Larsen & Luna propose that adolescent PFC development is governed by critical-period mechanisms, which would mean plasticity **narrows after adolescence**. This is a hypothesis supported by correspondence of mechanisms, not by human window-closure data.

### Inferences
- [D] In the retrieved evidence, the changes that can most plausibly be attributed to environment in typical children all have documented short-term reversibility at the behavioral level: sleep loss → irritability and inattention, and screen reduction → SDQ improvement. Stress-related PFC changes reversed in adult humans and rats. **Persistent, irreversible change is documented only for extreme deprivation within critical periods**, in animals and in institutionalized children.
- [D] No study has shown that ordinary screen exposure produces brain changes that persist after exposure ends. Equally, no study has shown that screen-associated brain correlates **reverse** after reduction, because no reduction trial measured the brain. The reversibility question for screens is therefore unanswered at the neural level.
- [D] If association-cortex plasticity is protracted, the same window that makes childhood and adolescence vulnerable also offers extended opportunity for recovery after environmental change. This is speculative.

### Gaps
- There are no RCTs of screen or social-media reduction in children with MRI, EEG or PET outcomes.
