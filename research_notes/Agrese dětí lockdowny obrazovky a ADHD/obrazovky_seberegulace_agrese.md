# Screen media use (by content type) and children's self-regulation, attention, emotion regulation and aggression: map of the evidence (0–18 years)

Scope and conventions. Compiled 2026-11-12. Literature 2004–2026, weighted to 2015–2026. Design tags: **[XS]** cross-sectional; **[L]** prospective/longitudinal (CLPM = cross-lagged panel model; RI-CLPM = random-intercept CLPM, which separates within-person change from stable between-person differences); **[E]** lab experiment; **[RCT]** randomized trial; **[NE]** natural or quasi-experiment; **[MA]/[SR]** meta-analysis/systematic review; **[WP]** working paper or preprint, not peer reviewed. Effect sizes are given exactly as reported (r, β, b, d, OR, RR). Rough benchmarks: r ≈ 0.10 is small and explains about 1% of variance; d ≈ 0.2 is small. Unless a source says otherwise, numbers come from abstracts retrieved via PubMed (NCBI) or Scite during this task. "Search-summary only" means the number came from a web-search digest of the primary source and the full text was not read. All 40 key DOIs were run through Scite `verify_papers` (20 + 20). Scite flagged no retraction notices. The only retraction found is the Chaarani 2022 retraction-and-replacement (see Q1), which came from a PubMed notice and which Scite did not flag.

## Key Question 1: Prospective and experimental evidence by media type (short-form video, gaming, social media, background TV, toddler tablet use) for attention, executive function (EF), inhibitory control, emotion regulation and aggression/externalizing

### Takeaway
Across media types, pooled associations with attention, EF and externalizing problems are consistently **small**: r ≈ 0.03–0.16 in children; b ≈ 0.06 in the largest longitudinal meta-analysis. They are often **bidirectional**, and longitudinal and experimental designs weaken them relative to cross-sectional ones. The exceptions with larger effects are (a) **gaming** in longitudinal socioemotional models, (b) **short-form video** correlations with attention/inhibition (r ≈ −0.38 to −0.41, but almost entirely cross-sectional, self-report and mostly in young adults), and (c) **content/context** effects in early childhood (age-inappropriate content, background TV, co-use). Experiments show short-lived EF dips after fantastical TV in preschoolers, and laboratory attention or prospective-memory costs after short-video exposure in adults. No child RCT tests attention or inhibitory control as a primary outcome.

### Inferences
- Effect sizes order roughly as: **content/context and use pattern** (age-inappropriate content, background TV, fantastical content, problematic/addictive use, gaming in longitudinal socioemotional models) **> total screen time**. This matches Xiao 2025, where baseline total screen time did not predict outcomes but addictive-use trajectories did (Q4).
- The early-childhood literature most consistently supports small prospective links (β ≈ 0.06–0.22) between screen exposure and later regulation difficulties. Because Fitzpatrick 2024, Vasconcellos 2025 and McDaniel & Radesky 2018 all find reverse paths of similar size, "screens cause dysregulation" cannot be separated from "dysregulated children get more screens".
- Gaming is the media type with the clearest two-way longitudinal signal for socioemotional problems (b 0.32/0.44). Yet experiments (Kühn 2019) and child-focused meta-analysis (Ferguson 2015, attention r=.03) show little effect on attention/EF. Problematic or heavy gaming, not gaming per se, is the more defensible risk marker.

### Gaps
- No child RCT with attention, inhibitory control or emotion regulation as a **primary** objectively measured outcome was found. SCREENS (Q6) used parent-reported SDQ.
- Under-12s are almost absent from short-form video research. No prospective SFV study with a child sample and objective attention tasks was found.
- Background-TV evidence on self-regulation beyond toddler play is meta-analytic and correlational. The child-level experiment (Schmidt 2008) is short-term and small.
- Burkhardt & Lenhard 2022 (Media Psychol 25(3):499–512, DOI 10.1080/15213269.2021.1980729; age-dependent longitudinal meta-analysis of violent games) was located, but its abstract was not retrievable, so no numbers are given.

## Key Question 2: Devices used as emotional pacifiers/regulators and later emotional reactivity; bidirectional (cross-lagged) effects

### Takeaway
Three lines of evidence point the same way, but all are parent-reported and from convenience or small samples:
- Radesky et al. 2023: device-calming predicted later emotional reactivity, **only in boys and high-surgency children**.
- Coyne et al. 2021: more media-based emotion regulation went with more problematic media use and more extreme emotions when media was removed, in cross-sectional plus observational data.
- Fitzpatrick et al. 2024: tablet use and anger formed a reciprocal cycle.

Effects are small, and the reverse direction (dysregulated child → more device use) is usually present too. "Loss of practice in self-regulation" is the authors' interpretation, not a tested mediator.

### Cited Findings
- **Radesky et al. 2023 [L, 3 waves over 6 mo, CLPM]**. Radesky JS, Kaciroti N, Weeks HM, Schaller A, Miller AL. *Longitudinal Associations Between Use of Mobile Devices for Calming and Emotional Reactivity and Executive Functioning in Children Aged 3 to 5 Years.* JAMA Pediatr 2023;177(1):62–70. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4793. US convenience sample N=422 (375 at T2, 366 at T3); mean age 3.8 y; 74.2% non-Hispanic White; 60.2% of parents with college degree. Exposure: parent-reported frequency of using devices to calm the child (5-point Likert). Outcomes: BRIEF-P Global Executive Composite and CBCL Emotional Reactivity.
  - **Boys:** device-calming T2 → emotional reactivity T3 **r(β)=0.20 (95% CI 0.10–0.30)**; the reverse path was **0.10 (−0.01 to 0.21), not significant**.
  - **High-surgency children:** calming → reactivity **0.11 (0.01–0.22)**; reactivity → calming **0.13 (0.02–0.24)**, i.e. bidirectional.
  - Authors: frequent device calming "may displace their opportunities for learning emotion-regulation strategies".
  - Limitations: subgroup-specific effects (sex, temperament median split); parent report for all measures; 6-month span; the EF finding is not reported as significant in the abstract. — [PubMed/JAMA Pediatr](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.4793)
  - Contrasting: a 2026 Behav Sci study found that shared, learning-oriented digital home activities did not show the negative pattern. It explains this as qualitatively different engagement. — [Scite contrasting citation, DOI 10.3390/bs16050740](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16050740)
- **Radesky et al. 2016 [XS research letter]**. Radesky JS, Peacock-Chambers E, Zuckerman B, Silverstein M. *Use of Mobile Technology to Calm Upset Children: Associations With Social-Emotional Development.* JAMA Pediatr 2016;170(4):397–399. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2015.4260. No abstract is available in PubMed and no results were retrieved; cited only as the precursor study. — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2015.4260)
- **Coyne et al. 2021 [XS + observational lab tasks]**. Temperament (negative affect, surgency) was associated with problematic media use, and media emotion regulation mediated this. Effect sizes not in the abstract; the design is cross-sectional. — [PubMed/CHB](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106762)
- **Fitzpatrick et al. 2024 [L, RI-CLPM]**: tablet use at 3.5 y → anger/frustration at 4.5 y β=0.22 (0.01–0.44), and anger at 4.5 y → tablet use at 5.5 y β=0.22 (0.01–0.43). Authors: "may contribute to a cycle that is deleterious for emotional regulation". — [PubMed/JAMA Pediatr](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.2511)
- **McDaniel & Radesky 2018 (longitudinal technoference) [L, 4 waves over 6 mo, CLPM]**. McDaniel BT, Radesky JS. *Technoference: longitudinal associations between parent technology use, parenting stress, and child behavior problems.* Pediatr Res 2018;84(2):210–218. DOI 10.1038/s41390-018-0052-6. 183 couples; children aged 0–5 (mean 3.0). Child externalizing → more parental technology interference, via parenting stress. Technology interference "often predicted" more externalizing. Bidirectional. — [PubMed/Pediatr Res](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-018-0052-6)
- **Vasconcellos et al. 2025 [MA of L]**: symmetrical reciprocal paths (b=0.06 each direction). Gaming has larger reciprocal paths (problems → gaming b=0.44, gaming → problems b=0.32). — [Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000468)
- **Li et al. 2025 [L, 2 waves, CLPM, adolescents]**. Li Y, Yang L, Lin S, Huang M, et al. *A digital Skinner box: The bidirectional longitudinal relationship between anxiety symptoms, delay of gratification, and short-form video addiction among Chinese adolescents.* J Res Adolesc 2025;35(4):e70098. DOI 10.1111/jora.70098. N=1,143 high-school students; mean age 15.87; 6-month lag. Anxiety → SFV addiction and SFV addiction → anxiety, with delay of gratification mediating both directions. Coefficients not in the abstract. Traditional CLPM, not RI-CLPM. — [Scite/JRA](https://doi.org/10.1111/jora.70098)

### Inferences
- The "digital pacifier" hypothesis has consistent directional support, but the evidence is thin: 3 small to moderate samples (N=269–422), predominantly white and educated (US/Canada), all parent-reported, with CIs whose lower bounds sit near zero.
- In Radesky 2023 and Fitzpatrick 2024 the reverse path is comparable in size. A transactional "vicious cycle" is therefore a better description than a one-way media effect, and the dysregulation-first path is just as well supported.
- Coyne's "extreme emotions when media was removed" is the closest early-childhood analogue to withdrawal-like reactions. It is cross-sectional, and the abstract did not establish whether it is distinct from ordinary tantrum proneness.

### Gaps
- No RCT tested an alternative calming strategy against device calming with emotion-regulation outcomes.
- No study with observer-rated or physiological emotion regulation (e.g. lab frustration tasks over time) as the longitudinal outcome of device calming was found.
- Coyne 2021 effect sizes were not available in the retrieved abstract.

## Key Question 3: Short-form video (SFV) "addiction" research (2021–2026, mostly Chinese samples): attention, inhibitory control, prospective memory, EEG/fMRI; quality appraisal

### Takeaway
SFV use or "addiction" scores correlate moderately with poorer self-reported and task-measured attention and inhibition: r ≈ −0.38 for attention and −0.41 for inhibitory control in the 2025 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis. These are the largest associations in this whole area. The quality of the base is low:
- About 88% of youth studies are cross-sectional (Ebster 2026).
- Most samples are Chinese university students.
- Scales such as the MPSVATQ and SFVA scales include attention-disruption items, which creates overlap between predictor and outcome.
- The few neuroimaging studies are small and cross-sectional, or show that baseline brain features predict later SFV addiction rather than the reverse.

Laboratory evidence of acute costs exists but comes from adults: prospective memory (Chiossi 2023) and boredom/attention after switching (Tam & Inzlicht 2024).

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Construct overlap:** SFVA/PSVU scales contain items about attentional disruption, loss of control and withdrawal (Shen 2026). Correlations between "addiction" scores and self-reported attention problems are therefore partly measurement overlap. This probably helps explain why the SFV effect sizes (r ≈ −.4) are several times larger than screen-time effects (r ≈ .1). Parry 2021 (Q7) also found problematic-use scales correlate weakly with logged use.
- **Direction:** the one longitudinal neuroimaging study (Liu 2025) shows brain and affective traits **predicting** later SFV addiction. It does not show SFV use altering the brain, so the imaging literature cannot yet support "SFV damages attention circuits".
- The acute experimental results (Chiossi; Tam & Inzlicht) support a plausible **state** mechanism: rapid context-switching costs prospective memory and sustained attention. No study shows that this accumulates into a **trait** deficit in children.

### Gaps
- No SFV study in children under 12 with objective attention tasks was found. No RCT reduced SFV use specifically in minors (confirmed by Kim 2026).
- CIs and the longitudinal/cross-sectional split for Nguyen 2025 were not available in the abstract. The full text was not retrieved.
- The psychometric validity of the main Chinese SFV scales (MPSVATQ, SVAS) was not appraised. The validation literature was not retrieved.

## Key Question 4: Problematic/addictive use (ICD-11 gaming disorder, problematic social media use scales); links with aggression, irritability and anger on device removal; withdrawal-like phenomena; quality of evidence

### Takeaway
"Problematic" or "addictive" use, rather than time spent, is the exposure most consistently linked to externalizing, irritability and ADHD symptoms (Thorell 2024; Xiao 2025). ICD-11 gaming disorder (6C51) is defined by impaired control, increasing priority and continuation despite harm, with significant impairment, normally over 12 months.

The evidence on withdrawal is weak. Irritability and restlessness are the most commonly cited withdrawal features, but the systematic review called the evidence "very underdeveloped". A small abstinence study found negative affect **declined** during abstinence. Prevalence estimates depend heavily on the screening instrument. Several longitudinal studies show aggression or academic problems **preceding** problematic gaming.

### Cited Findings

It must cause significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational or occupational functioning. The pattern is normally evident over **at least 12 months**, which may be shortened if all requirements are met and symptoms are severe. — [RACGP AJGP 2026 summary](https://www1.racgp.org.au/ajgp/2026/may/gaming-disorder); [CISMeF ICD-11 entry](https://www.cismef.org/page/detail/en/ICD11_CA_1448597234). *The official WHO pages (who.int) could not be fetched in this session; see Gaps.*

### Inferences
- Of all exposure types, "problematic use" correlates most robustly with irritability, aggression and inattention. The only prospective abstinence data (adults, n=24) show **improvement**, not worsening. - No meta-analysis specific to problematic social media use and aggression or irritability in minors was retrieved.

## Key Question 5: Media violence and aggression: meta-analyses, the scientific dispute, and frustration/competence-impediment accounts

### Takeaway
All camps now agree the average association between violent game exposure and later aggression is **small**. Estimates in youth longitudinal data run from β≈0.08–0.11 (Prescott 2018, controlling for baseline aggression) down to r≈0.06, and r≈0.01 (non-significant) for the best-designed studies (Drummond 2020). The disputes are about publication bias, quality criteria, researcher expectancy, and whether small means trivial.

Mathur & VanderWeele (2019) argue the competing meta-analyses agree that effects are almost always small. An alternative account, frustration or competence impediment rather than violent content, has experimental support in adults (Przybylski 2014). A direct replication failed, while a VR extension partly supported frustration (Ferguson et al. 2021). A natural experiment with adults found violent movies **reduce** same-day assaults through incapacitation.

DOI 10.1037/a0018251. Significant effects on all 6 outcomes. ### Inferences
- The defensible summary for children: prospective associations between violent gaming and later physical aggression are about β 0.06–0.11. They shrink further with covariates, better design and longer lags, and are statistically zero in the best-practice subset of one meta-analysis. They are not zero in another (Prescott). Neither side has shown a clinically meaningful population effect.
- The frustration/competence account fits better with findings that game **difficulty or failure**, not violent content, drives short-term hostility. That is relevant to "rage-quitting" and tablet-removal anger, but the evidence comes from adults, and one direct replication failed.
- Aggregate natural experiments (Dahl & DellaVigna; violent-crime trends) speak to adult population crime, not to children's everyday aggression or irritability.

### Gaps
- Pooled numeric effect sizes for Anderson 2010 and Burkhardt & Lenhard 2022 were not retrieved.

## Key Question 6: Causal evidence: RCTs and experiments reducing screen time, digital-detox trials, school phone-ban evaluations, natural experiments

### Takeaway
- **RCT evidence in minors is minimal.** One Danish family trial (SCREENS: 2 weeks, 89 families) found improved parent-rated SDQ difficulties (d=0.53, mostly internalizing and prosocial) and +46 min/day of physical activity, but no EEG-measured sleep change. It was unblinded and short.
- **Social-media abstinence experiments**, mostly with adults, pool to approximately zero (Ferguson 2024). A disputed re-analysis suggests that interventions of 1 week or longer help (d≈0.16).
- **Adult RCTs** show that blocking mobile internet improves objectively measured sustained attention (Castelo 2025).
- **School phone restrictions:** the best English study (SMART Schools, cross-sectional) found **no** wellbeing difference. Norwegian event-study evidence of benefits for girls is a contested working paper. No RCTs exist.
- **Natural experiments on broadband and social-media rollouts** suggest adverse mental-health effects in youth or students (internalizing and hospitalization outcomes, not attention or aggression). The introduction of TV had no negative effect on later test scores.

### Inferences
- The only child RCT shows that a radical, short (2-week) whole-family screen reduction improves **parent-rated** emotional and peer problems and prosocial behaviour. It does not test attention, inhibitory control or aggression with objective measures, and lasting effects are unknown. Unblinded parent report is the main threat, the same weakness Ferguson raises for adult abstinence experiments.
- School restrictions reduce in-school phone time but, in England, not total use or wellbeing. Any benefit for attention or behaviour would have to come from in-class distraction effects (academic gains for low achievers in Beland & Murphy). These have not been directly measured in recent trials.
- Natural experiments point to adverse effects of connectivity on internalizing problems and hospitalizations in youth. None measures attention, EF or aggression, so they cannot be transferred directly to self-regulation outcomes.

### Gaps
- No RCT or natural experiment was found with **attention, inhibitory control, emotion regulation or aggression** in minors as a primary objective outcome.
- No randomized evaluation of school phone bans exists (Campbell 2024).
- Exact pooled d for Ferguson 2024 and the main regression tables for Braghieri, Donati and Abrahamsson were not retrieved.

## Key Question 7: Critical and skeptical literature: magnitude, Odgers 2024 vs Haidt, Goldilocks, measurement error, reverse causation, confounding

### Takeaway
1. Associations between total digital use and youth wellbeing are tiny, at most 0.4% of variance in specification-curve analyses.
2. Links are often non-linear: moderate use is not harmful (Goldilocks).
3. Self-reported time correlates only about r≈0.38 with device logs, and problematic-use scales even less.
4. Reverse paths are common and comparable in size.
5. Several high-profile findings did not survive reanalysis or replication: Christakis 2004 under Foster & Watkins 2010, Przybylski 2014 Study 6 under Ferguson 2021, and Chaarani 2022 was retracted and replaced.

Critics (Odgers) argue that claims of a social-media-driven mental-illness epidemic are "not supported by science". Proponents (Haidt; Schmidt-Persson's trial authors) point to experimental effects of moderate size. Small does not mean irrelevant, but the burden of causal proof remains largely unmet for self-regulation outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **Orben & Przybylski 2019 [specification-curve analysis, 3 datasets, n=355,358]**. *The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use.* Nat Hum Behav 2019;3(2):173–182. DOI 10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1. The association is "negative but small, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being". The authors judge these effects "too small to warrant policy change". — [PubMed/NHB](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1)
  - Contrasting: Schmidt-Persson 2024 argues that its trial effect (d=0.53) contrasts with "negligible" observational estimates. — [Scite citation](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.19881)
- **Przybylski & Weinstein 2017 [preregistered, n=120,115 English adolescents]**. *A Large-Scale Test of the Goldilocks Hypothesis.* Psychol Sci 2017;28(2):204–215. DOI 10.1177/0956797616678438. Screen time–wellbeing links are quadratic and differ between weekdays and weekends. "moderate use of digital technology is not intrinsically harmful and may be advantageous". — [PubMed/Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616678438)
  - Contrasting: a 2025 Acta Paediatrica study found the same curvilinear pattern but "did not observe a J-shaped function" (no benefit at zero use). — [Scite, DOI 10.1111/apa.70524](https://doi.org/10.1111/apa.70524)
- **Parry et al. 2021 [MA, measurement]**. Parry DA, Davidson BI, Sewall CJR, et al. *A systematic review and meta-analysis of discrepancies between logged and self-reported digital media use.* Nat Hum Behav 2021;5(11):1535–1547. - **Reverse causation (direct estimates):**
  - Vasconcellos 2025: b=0.06 in both directions; gaming 0.44 (problems → gaming) vs 0.32 (gaming → problems). — [Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000468)

- **Researcher expectancy and citation bias:**
  - Drummond 2020: effects were lower where there was less evidence of researcher expectancy. — [RSOS](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.200373)
  - Ferguson 2024: studies with citation bias produced higher effect sizes. — [PPM](https://doi.org/10.1037/ppm0000541)
- **Retraction/replacement:** Chaarani 2022 (gaming → better inhibition) was retracted and replaced (JAMA Netw Open 2023;6(4):e236895). — [PubMed notice](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.6895)
- **Small-effects editorial:** Coyne, Swit & Holmgren 2025. *Parental Technoference—Small Effects, Big Questions.* JAMA Pediatr 2025;179(7):699. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.0679. ### Inferences
- When reading any headline effect, check five things: design (cross-sectional vs within-person longitudinal), informant (parent-rated outcome and exposure), exposure metric (self-reported time vs logs vs "problematic use" scales), covariate set, and whether the reverse path was modelled. The largest effects (SFV r≈−.4; problematic-use correlations) come from the weakest designs and the most overlapping measures.
- Measurement error in self-reported time (r≈.38 with logs) could attenuate true time effects. Errors that are systematic, for example related to wellbeing (as the Parry preprint discussion notes), could also bias them in either direction. "Small" observational estimates are therefore not decisive evidence of small true effects. They are also not evidence of large ones.

### Gaps
- The full text of Odgers 2024 was not retrieved (nature.com blocked), so her specific arguments are known only from secondary sources.
- Haidt and Rausch's compilation of experiments was not retrieved or evaluated.
- No large preregistered specification-curve analysis focused specifically on attention/EF or aggression in children was found (Orben & Przybylski covered wellbeing).

## Key Question 8: Proposed psychological mechanisms: displacement (sleep, free play, physical activity, parent–child interaction), arousal/hyperstimulation, variable-ratio reinforcement and design features, technoference, lost practice in frustration tolerance and boredom

### Takeaway
**Displacement** has the best causal support for **physical activity**: +46 min/day in the SCREENS RCT. The effect on **sleep** is mixed: strong cross-sectional association, but a null result in the RCT's EEG measures. Disruption of toddler **play** is supported experimentally.

**Technoference** shows small but consistent associations with child externalizing problems (r≈0.15) and bidirectional dynamics, but a large multinational study was null for inhibition.

**Hyperstimulation:** experiments implicate fantastical content, not editing pace. Infant EEG theta/beta findings are correlational.

**Reward-learning or design accounts** (variable rewards, infinite scroll, personalization) are supported in adults (posting behaviour fits reinforcement learning; switching increases boredom) but are untested as developmental mechanisms in children.

**Lost practice in self-regulation** (device calming displacing coping, low boredom tolerance) is an authors' interpretation without direct mediation tests.

**Displacement**
- Physical activity: SCREENS RCT, +45.8 min/day (27.9–63.6) of leisure non-sedentary activity in children. — [Pedersen 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.1519)
- Sleep:
  - SCREENS RCT: "No significant between-group mean differences … for the electroencephalography-based sleep outcomes". — [Pedersen 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2022.1519)

Carter B, Rees P, Hale L, Bhattacharjee D, Paradkar MS. JAMA Pediatr 2016;170(12):1202–1208. DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341. — [PubMed](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)

### Inferences
- The mechanisms with the strongest designs are **displacement of physical activity** (RCT) and **acute content effects on EF** (experiments). The mechanisms most often invoked in public debate, dopamine/variable-ratio "rewiring" of attention in children and loss of boredom tolerance, have the weakest direct developmental evidence.
- The pattern across studies suggests a **transactional model**: temperamentally reactive children and stressed or distracted parents use devices more as regulators, and that use may then reduce regulation practice and parent–child interaction. This fits the bidirectional estimates better than unidirectional "screen harm" or pure-selection accounts. It is inferred from convergent small effects, not directly tested.

### Gaps
- No study formally tested sleep or physical activity as **mediators** of screen effects on attention or aggression in children with objective measures and within-person designs.
- No developmental (child) study tested variable-ratio or infinite-scroll design features experimentally on attention or self-control.
- Montag et al. and other design-feature reviews were not retrieved.
- Christakis's "overstimulation hypothesis" papers beyond the 2004/2007 cohorts were not retrieved.
