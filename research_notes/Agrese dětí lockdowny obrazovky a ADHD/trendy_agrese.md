## 1. Czech Republic: what do official statistics show for child (<15) and juvenile (15-17) violent offending?

### Takeaway
Over the long term, registered offending by Czech children and juveniles has collapsed. In 2000 there were 8,899 children and 8,905 juveniles among investigated or prosecuted persons. By 2024-2025 there were roughly 1,700 children and 2,350 juveniles, while the 10-14 age cohort grew by about 30% after 2010. IKSP found a significant decline 2008-2022, including per 10,000 children. Within the low post-2021 level the picture is mixed:
- Total counts rebounded after the COVID trough of 2020-2021 to roughly the 2018-2019 level. They did not exceed it, and the child count fell slightly in 2024 and 2025.
- Prosecutors (NSZ) report a qualitative and compositional shift toward violence. Violent "provinění" (bodily harm, robbery) by juveniles in 2024-2025 were about 40% above the 2015-2019 average, and robberies by under-15s rose similarly.
- IKSP's own 2020-2023 research explicitly did not confirm that children's aggression or brutality is rising.

### Inferences
- **(a) Long-term trend (2000-2019):** a large decline. Child plus juvenile offenders went from about 17,800 (2000) to about 4,300 (2018-2019), while the 10-14 cohort grew after 2010. This matches the international "youth crime drop" (see Q2/Q5).
- **(b) Smartphone era (2012-2019):** no sign of a rise in Czech registered child or juvenile offending. IKSP describes stabilisation after an early-period decline. The data from this window are thin in what I could retrieve, and year-by-year 2010-2017 figures were not obtained.
- **(c) COVID (2020-2021):** a sharp, mechanically expected trough (schools closed, less street time). Any later comparison that uses 2020-2021 as a baseline overstates growth.
- **(d) Post-pandemic (2022-2025):**
  - Counts returned to roughly the 2018-2019 level and then plateaued or fell slightly: children 1,822 → 1,679; juveniles 1,894 → 2,351, flat 2023-2025.
  - Per child aged 10-14, 2024 is below 2019.
  - The signal for a "rise" rests on composition (more bodily harm and robbery, NSZ's "+40% vs 2015-2019" for juveniles) and on qualitative prosecutor observations, not on total volume.
  - Composition shifts can be partly artefactual, because property crime was mechanically removed in 2020 (see Q4).
- The NSZ "+40%" claim and the IKSP "no rise in aggression" conclusion cover different windows. IKSP's data run to about 2022; NSZ compares 2024-2025 with 2015-2019. Both can be true: no long-run rise, but a recent uptick in violent juvenile cases relative to the late-2010s low.

## 2. Comparators (Germany, UK, Sweden, Netherlands, USA) and cross-national surveys (HBSC, including Czech data)

### Takeaway
Every comparator shows the same long-run pattern: a large fall in youth violence from the mid-1990s or 2000s to the mid-to-late 2010s, in both police data and self-report surveys. HBSC self-reports across 19 countries, including the Czech Republic, show declining fighting among boys and declining bullying from 1994/2002 to 2022, with no pandemic break except for cyberbullying.

The post-2016 and post-pandemic picture diverges by country:
- **Germany:** police-recorded violence by children (<14) has risen clearly since 2016. The child violent-suspect rate doubled from 138 to 288 per 100,000 between 2016 and 2025. Juveniles rose until 2024 and then fell 7.4% in 2025. Self-report data (Lower Saxony) do not confirm the police rise.
- **England & Wales:** first-time entrants are far below 2014, but homicide convictions of 10-17-year-olds, knife-crime disposals and school suspensions for assault rose.
- **Sweden:** an organised-crime-driven rise in under-15 involvement in serious violence, while shootings fell sharply and self-reported violence fell.
- **Netherlands:** flat at a low level, more than halved versus 2006-2008.
- **USA:** juvenile violent arrests fell more than 60% since 2006, dropped in 2020, partly rebounded, and were still below 2019 in 2024. Self-reported fighting fell from 42.5% (1991) to about 18-19% (2021-2023).

### Cited Findings
**Cross-national: HBSC**
- [PR] Molcho et al. 2025 (*Int J Public Health* 70, DOI 10.3389/ijph.2025.1607654):
  - Data: 8 HBSC cycles 1994-2022, 789,531 children aged 11/13/15, 19 countries **including the Czech Republic** (also Austria, Belgium regions, Canada, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greenland, Hungary, Israel, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Scotland, Sweden, Wales).
  - Frequent fighting (≥3 fights in 12 months) among boys fell:
    - age 11: 27.4% (2002) → 18.9% (2022)
    - age 13: 22.4% → 13.6%
    - age 15: 18.1% → 10.6%
    - all p<0.001
  - Bullying perpetration among boys fell:
    - age 11: 23.8% (1994) → 6.5% (2022)
    - age 13: 27.5% → 6.9%
    - age 15: 27.2% → 8.1%
  - Girls' fighting stayed low and stable, or rose. Cyberbullying rose 2018 → 2022 in most groups, to about 1 in 6 victimised in 2022.
  - Conclusion: "a decline in traditional forms of adolescent violence."
  - Sources: [doi.org/10.3389/ijph.2025.1607654](https://doi.org/10.3389/ijph.2025.1607654); [PMC11891013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11891013/)

  - HBSC 2013/14-2021/22, more than 700,000 students, 40+ countries.

  - Physical fighting fell among 15-year-old boys and rose among girls aged 11 and 13.
  - Cyberbullying increased post-pandemic at ages 11 and 13.
  - Conclusion: "Except for cyberbullying, the pandemic did not appear to influence trends in peer violence and bullying."
  - Sources: [doi.org/10.3390/bs16010036](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16010036); [PMC12838183](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12838183/)

- The best-supported cross-national statement is that **traditional peer violence (fighting, bullying) is lower in 2022 than in any earlier HBSC cycle, and the pandemic did not break that trend**, with the exception of cyberbullying.
- Where police data show post-2016 or post-2021 rises (Germany children, England homicide and knife, Sweden under-15 serious crime), they are concentrated in **small, extreme or organised-crime-linked subgroups**, or in young children's police-recorded suspicion, not in population-wide self-reported violence.
- Germany is the most important comparator for a genuine rise in recorded child violence. But Germany's own self-report survey contradicts the police trend for adolescents, which points toward reporting and recording effects (see Q4).

- No 2024-2025 data yet from HBSC. The 2025/26 cycle is not published.
- German PKS series by age for 2005-2023 (exact TVBZ by year) was only partly retrieved.

## 3. Teacher- and school-reported behaviour problems after the pandemic, and clinical referrals for conduct problems and irritability

### Takeaway
School-staff reports in the US, England, Germany and Czechia consistently describe more disruptive, disrespectful and aggressive behaviour after school reopening (2021/22 onward), and record-high suspensions for assault in England (2023/24). Some indicators then eased:
- English teachers' ratings of behaviour improved sharply by May 2025.
- English autumn 2024/25 suspensions fell 3%.

These are perception or administrative-response measures, sensitive to staffing, thresholds and attention. They do not map directly onto rates of physical violence. Clinical evidence points to raised mental-health problems and ADHD-type symptoms post-pandemic, with general distress still about 5 percentage points above pre-pandemic in Germany in 2024. I found no robust time series on clinical referrals specifically for conduct problems or aggression.

### Inferences
- Staff-reported disruption and disrespect rose after reopening (2021/22-2023/24) in the US and England. In England, behaviour ratings and autumn-term suspensions improved by 2024/25. This fits a pandemic-shock-and-partial-recovery pattern more than a continuous smartphone-era trend, though the data cannot separate the two.
- Rising suspensions for assault in England (2021/22 → 2023/24) are an administrative response measure. They can rise because of behaviour-policy tightening ("zero tolerance"), staff shortages, or changes in reason coding, as well as because of more assaults. The HBSC self-report data (2022) show no rise in fighting.
- Clinical data support increased emotional problems, inattention and hyperactivity during and after the pandemic. Evidence specifically for increased aggression or conduct disorder is weaker and heterogeneous.

### Gaps
- No time series found on clinical referrals or diagnoses for conduct disorder, ODD or irritability (ICD F91/F92) in Czechia (ÚZIS, NUDZ) or elsewhere. This needs ÚZIS child-psychiatry reports or the NUDZ child mental-health monitoring.
- Czech teacher surveys with repeated measures of aggression toward teachers (e.g., ČŠI thematic reports, union surveys) were not found.
- NCES 2024/25 School Pulse behaviour items and the NCES "Indicators of School Crime and Safety" (2024/2025 editions) were not retrieved; nces.ed.gov is blocked.
- Preschool (age 3-6) aggression trends: no population-level time series found for any country. The Kindred² survey covers readiness, not aggression.

## 4. Data artifacts: reporting, definitions, legal reforms, police practice, population, migration, age of responsibility, and media perception vs measured rates

### Takeaway
Several documented artifacts can produce an apparent recent rise in child and juvenile violence without any change in behaviour:
- **Czech Republic:** the 2020 doubling of the property-damage threshold, which removes petty theft and raises violence's share; the use of the 2020-2021 COVID trough as a baseline; growth of the 10-14 cohort by about 30% since 2010; heightened reporting of school threats after December 2023; and a 2026 change in police recording of school incidents.
- **Germany:** Census-2022 rebasing of rates; a strong police "Hellfeld" rise that the self-report survey does not confirm.
- **Sweden:** legal and procedural changes targeting under-15s.
- **Generally:** suspension and exclusion counts reflect school policy.

Official Czech experts (IKSP) explicitly state that the "rising child aggression" narrative is not supported by their data.

### Cited Findings
**Czech Republic**
- [OFF/legal] Act No. 333/2020 Coll., effective 1 Oct 2020, doubled the damage thresholds in § 138 of the Criminal Code. "Škoda nikoli nepatrná" rose from CZK 5,000 to 10,000, so many thefts became misdemeanours handled by municipalities. Critics said this would "artificially reduce" registered crime. — [Právní prostor: hranice škody se zvyšuje na dvojnásobek](https://pravniprostor.cz/clanky/trestni-pravo/hranice-skody-se-v-trestnepravnich-kauzach-zvysuje-na-dvojnasobek); [Ekonomický deník](https://ekonomickydenik.cz/svaz-mest-obci-zvyseni-hranice-trestneho-cinu-navali-obce-dalsi-agendu-prestupku/)

### Inferences
- The Czech 2020 threshold change removes a chunk of petty property crime, much of it committed by juveniles. That mechanically increases the **share** of violent offences among recorded youth offences after October 2020, even if violent behaviour is constant. Claims that youth crime is "shifting toward violence" (Bradáčová, NSZ) are partly vulnerable to this artifact. NSZ's absolute comparison of violent offences versus 2015-2019 (+40% for juveniles) is not affected by it, but it is affected by cohort growth (10-14 in 2015 → 15-17 by 2020-2025) and by reporting sensitivity.
- Reporting sensitivity plausibly rose in Czechia after December 2023 (school-threat awareness), after the 2024 radicalisation reports, and with ongoing media attention to "zážitkové násilí". Victim surveys or self-report surveys are needed to separate behaviour from reporting. The only one available here is HBSC 2022, which shows decline.
- The perception-reality gap is documented directly by IKSP for Czechia and implicitly in Germany (police vs survey divergence).

### Gaps
- No quantitative Czech study was found that measures media coverage volume of youth violence against measured rates.
- The impact of the Act 333/2020 threshold change on juvenile and child offence composition has not been quantified anywhere I found.
- No data were found on offending by Ukrainian refugee minors in Czechia; this should not be assumed either way.
- The effect of the 2024 Youth Justice Act amendment on under-15 case handling and statistics is not yet evaluated.

## 5. Alternative explanations in the literature for any observed rise (and for the long decline)

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed literature is much stronger on explaining the long **decline** than on explaining any recent rise. The decline is explained mainly by fewer unstructured, unsupervised offline routine activities with peers, less alcohol use, stronger social bonds and more anti-crime attitudes, with time online as an indirect contributor.

Explanations for recent localised rises are mostly qualitative, official-commentary, or case-study based. They include:
- social-media-mediated conflict escalation (Patton et al.)
- organised-crime recruitment of minors (Sweden)
- weapon availability (England knife possession)
- parental and family stress and mental-health service gaps
- cannabis use and unsupervised time (Oslo)
- "thrill" violence (NSZ, Czechia)
- online radicalisation (MV, Czechia)
- declining youth-service spending (England)

None is established as a causal driver of a population-level rise.

### Cited Findings
**Explaining the long decline**
- [PR] Svensson & Oberwittler 2021 (*Criminology* 59(2):351-386, DOI 10.1111/1745-9125.12273): Swedish national school survey 1999-2017, about 49,000 respondents. Changes in social bonds, attitudes toward crime and routine activities were all associated with the decline in youth crime. Routine activities, specifically less unstructured socialising, and less binge drinking had the strongest explanatory power. Together the predictors accounted for most of the decline. — [doi.org/10.1111/1745-9125.12273](https://doi.org/10.1111/1745-9125.12273)
- [PR] Same paper, on mechanisms:
  - The digital revolution as a cause of the decline is a "widely discussed assumption" (Arnett 2018; Weerman 2017).
  - Their finding on declining unstructured socialising "could be seen as an indirect support".
  - Twenge & Park (2019) note that the decline of some routine activities started before Internet use spread, so the Internet "can only be a partial explanation".
  - In Germany and the Netherlands the police-recorded decline started around 2007; in the US, Spain and the Nordic countries self-report data suggest a decline from the mid-1990s.
  - Source: [doi.org/10.1111/1745-9125.12273](https://doi.org/10.1111/1745-9125.12273)
- [PR] Oberwittler & Svensson 2025, "The International Youth Crime Drop: Evidence and Explanations" (*Crime and Justice* 54:153-216, DOI 10.1086/737409): a review. Only metadata was retrieved; the full text was not read. — [doi.org/10.1086/737409](https://doi.org/10.1086/737409); [OA handle](https://hdl.handle.net/21.11116/0000-0013-51BC-C)
- [PR] McCarthy 2020, "How Universal is the Youth Crime Drop?" (*Victims & Offenders* 16(6), DOI 10.1080/15564886.2020.1855281) questions uniformity across socio-economic groups. Only metadata was retrieved. — [doi.org/10.1080/15564886.2020.1855281](https://doi.org/10.1080/15564886.2020.1855281)
- [PR] Molcho et al. 2025 propose several explanations for the HBSC decline:
  - anti-bullying programmes
  - changing social norms, with a possible "floor effect" leaving individual and proximal factors (self-regulation, family) as more important
  - a parallel decline in alcohol, tobacco and cannabis use
  - growing gender equality narrowing the boy-girl gap
  - speculatively, declining testosterone
  - Source: [doi.org/10.3389/ijph.2025.1607654](https://doi.org/10.3389/ijph.2025.1607654)

**Candidate drivers of recent localised rises**
- [PR] Oslo 2015 → 2018: the rise in self-reported fighting was statistically related to more time unsupervised by adults, rising digital media use and rising cannabis use. Parental supervision and organised leisure moved opposite to fighting and did not explain it. — [doi.org/10.1007/s10964-020-01252-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-020-01252-8)
- [PR, qualitative] Social media and offline violence:
  - Patton, Pyrooz, Decker et al. 2019 (*Int J Bullying Prev*, DOI 10.1007/s42380-019-00014-w): 17 gang-involved Black males aged 14-24 in Chicago identified three online forms ("dissing", "calling", direct threats) that can escalate to offline violence.
  - Patton et al. 2019 (*Palgrave Communications*, DOI 10.1057/s41599-019-0330-x): outreach workers report social media "plays an increasing role in escalation of conflict between rival gangs".
  - Snyder et al. 2021 (CrimRxiv, DOI 10.21428/cb6ab371.8a624211): youth focus groups name social-media threats or disrespect as one of three common precursors to violence.
  - These are small qualitative US samples and not evidence of population-level trends.
  - Sources: [doi.org/10.1007/s42380-019-00014-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42380-019-00014-w); [doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0330-x](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0330-x); [doi.org/10.21428/cb6ab371.8a624211](https://doi.org/10.21428/cb6ab371.8a624211)
- [OFF via NEWS] Organised-crime recruitment (Sweden): the growth in under-15 suspects for murder plots runs in parallel with falling shootings. ### Inferences
- The routine-activity explanation of the decline (less unsupervised offline peer time) implies two things:
  - The smartphone era should depress opportunity-based offline violence, which is consistent with HBSC and self-report declines through 2022.
  - It could raise online aggression (cyberbullying ↑ 2018-2022) and occasional online-to-offline escalation in specific subgroups.
- The pandemic (shock to family stress, schooling and services) plus a rebound in street time after reopening is a simpler explanation for a 2022-2024 uptick in recorded incidents than a continuous smartphone effect. But no study retrieved here tests this formally for Czechia.
- For Czechia, the competing explanations that need testing against data are:
  1. composition artifact from Act 333/2020;
  2. cohort growth;
  3. reporting sensitivity after 2023;
  4. genuine post-pandemic increase in violent juvenile offending, as NSZ claims;
  5. a small, concentrated "thrill violence" or radicalised subgroup.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests the smartphone or social-media hypothesis against recorded youth violence trends in Czechia or the Central European region.
- The full text of Oberwittler & Svensson 2025, the most relevant review, was not read; only metadata was retrieved.
- Evidence on substance use (e.g., ESPAD Czech trends) as a driver was not searched in this task.
- Contagion or imitation of violence online (e.g., filmed fights, school-shooting copycats) appears in Czech official commentary, but no peer-reviewed trend evidence was retrieved.
- Tools not used:
  - The Consensus search was blocked by its usage quota.
  - WebFetch was blocked for all primary-statistics hosts.
  - A PubMed query "juvenile violence COVID-19 pandemic trends arrests post-pandemic increase" returned 0 hits. It was too restrictive because of AND-ed terms. A broader PubMed query (externalizing/conduct problems + COVID + longitudinal/meta-analysis) returned 19 hits, mostly small cohort studies.
