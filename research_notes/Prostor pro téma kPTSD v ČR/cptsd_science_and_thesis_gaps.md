## 1. ICD-11 definition, and why DSM-5/DSM-5-TR does not include CPTSD

### Takeaway
ICD-11 CPTSD = the three core PTSD clusters (re-experiencing, avoidance, sense of threat) plus three "disturbances in self-organization" (DSO): affective dysregulation, negative self-concept and disturbed relationships. It typically follows sustained or multiple trauma, but childhood trauma is not required. DSM-5 keeps a single, broader PTSD diagnosis that already absorbs several DSO-like symptoms. No DSM move toward a separate CPTSD diagnosis was found in this search.

### Cited Findings
- CPTSD is "characterised by three core post-traumatic symptom clusters, along with chronic and pervasive disturbances in emotion regulation, identity, and relationships". People with it "typically have sustained or multiple exposures to trauma, such as childhood abuse and domestic or community violence" (Maercker, Cloitre et al., *Lancet* 2022) [CURRENT] — [Maercker et al. 2022, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(22)00821-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(22)00821-2)
- The three core PTSD clusters (re-experiencing, avoidance, sense of threat) plus three DSO (negative self-concept, disturbances in relationships, affective dysregulation) make up CPTSD (Zhang et al. 2026, systematic review of network studies) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103234](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103234)
- The trauma is described as "severe, prolonged and repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible" (Melkam et al. 2026) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2735202](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2735202)
- ICD-11 has been "in use globally since 2022" (Ahern et al. 2026) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2685933](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2685933)
- The CPTSD and PTSD labels were proposed in ICD-11 as two "distinct sibling conditions" (Karatzias et al. 2017/2018) [FOUNDATIONAL] — [DOI 10.1080/20008198.2017.1418103](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008198.2017.1418103)
- "The DSM-5 conceptualises PTSD broadly, whereas the ICD-11 distinguishes PTSD from complex PTSD." In 585 clinical and 2,146 school adolescents, DSM-5 PTSD prevalence was "substantially higher" than ICD-11 PTSD, and "DSM-5 PTSD aligned more closely with ICD-11 CPTSD than with ICD-11 PTSD" (Xu et al. 2026) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2685473](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2685473)
- A BJPsych Advances review reports that DSM-5 PTSD is broader and includes many symptoms that belong to both ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD. This is a search-snippet-level citation; the full text was not read — [Cambridge Core PDF](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/2977140CBDAAF402610715BB609F688C/S2056467819000483a.pdf/complex_posttraumatic_stress_disorder_a_new_diagnosis_in_icd11.pdf)
- Earlier global clinician surveys found CPTSD was the diagnosis most often suggested for inclusion in ICD-11, mainly by psychologists. Vignette field studies found that adding CPTSD improved diagnostic decisions compared with ICD-10. This is a search-snippet summary of [PMC9881112, "The ICD-11 Diagnoses in the Mental Health Field"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9881112/); the primary studies were not opened.

### Inferences
- In practice, CPTSD in DSM terms is coded as PTSD plus comorbidity (often BPD, depression or dissociation). This is inference. It matters in CZ because Czech clinicians are moving from ICD-10 (where only F62.0 "enduring personality change after catastrophic experience" existed) to ICD-11. The F62.0 point comes from general knowledge and was not verified in this session.
- "Childhood trauma is required" is a misconception. The ICD-11 stressor is defined by being prolonged or repeated and hard to escape (e.g. torture, domestic violence, war), not by age.

### Gaps
- The exact WHO ICD-11 6B41 text (including the stressor examples and the "functional impairment" requirement) was not retrieved because icd.who.int was blocked. The student should quote it directly from icd.who.int.
- No 2025–2026 source on any APA/DSM work group plan to add CPTSD (e.g. to a DSM-6) was found. Status unknown.
- The Czech implementation timeline for ICD-11 (ÚZIS / MKN-11 Czech translation) was not researched.

## 2. Prevalence of ICD-11 CPTSD (general, clinical, European / CEE)

### Takeaway
Pooled community estimates run from about 4% to 9%, depending on the review. Clinical estimates are about 45%. In general-population samples, CPTSD is often more common than PTSD. CEE data, including Czech respondents, exist from 2026, but self-report ITQ inflates rates by roughly a factor of two compared with the new "clinical checks" version.

### Cited Findings
- **Lancet seminar:** "1–8% population prevalence and up to 50% prevalence in mental health facilities" [CURRENT] — [Maercker et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(22)00821-2)
- **Huynh et al. 2025** (Psychiatry Research): 167 studies, N = 138,681. Global pooled CPTSD = **6.2% (95% CI 3.7–10.3%)**. Clinical samples **44.7%**, domestic violence/sexual abuse survivors **40.0%**, military **36.4%**, emergency services **7.4%**. "No gender difference in prevalence was observed." [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.psychres.2025.116586](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2025.116586)
- **Fung et al. 2025** (Asian J Psychiatry, pre-registered, 10 representative general-population studies, all using ITQ): non-war/economically developed samples (N = 7,718) pooled **PTSD 2%, CPTSD 4%**. War-exposed/less developed samples (N = 9,870) pooled **PTSD 16%, CPTSD 15%**. The countries included Ukraine, Germany, Ireland, Hong Kong, Israel, Kenya, Nigeria and Ghana; there were no CZ data [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.ajp.2025.104610](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajp.2025.104610)
- **Hualparuca-Olivera et al. 2025** (Int J Soc Psychiatry): 16 studies, 22 effect sizes. One-month community prevalence **8.59% (95% CI 5.84–11.34%)**, I² = 98.6%. Prevalence varied by continent, scale, sample type, country and language [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1177/00207640251368064](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640251368064)
- **Refugees/displaced (Melkam et al. 2026)**: 17 samples, N = 12,504, range 3.0–74.6%. Pooled prevalence **20.9% (95% CI 12.6–32.6%)**, prediction interval 1.7–80.3%. Community samples 10.6% vs treatment-seeking samples 55.2%. Post-migration living difficulties were associated more with DSO than with core PTSD [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2735202](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2735202)
- **Military/veterans (Grinsill et al. 2024)**: 16 studies. In 13 of them CPTSD was more prevalent than PTSD. CPTSD ranged 5–80.63% and PTSD 3.8–42.37%; too heterogeneous for meta-analysis [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1177/15248380241246996](https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380241246996)
- **Central/Eastern Europe including the Czech Republic (Levin, …, Preiss, …, Ben-Ezra 2026, Psychiatry Research)**: 5,070 adults from five CEE countries (CZ, PL, HU, RO, SK). Of these, 4,012 (79.1%) reported trauma and completed the ITQ. "CPTSD consistently exceeded PTSD", and Poland was highest (CPTSD 23.6%, PTSD 14.4%). Negative self-concept was the most central symptom. "Czech Republic and Slovakia uniquely showed additional central PTSD symptoms." [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117303](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2026.117303). *Note:* the Czech-specific prevalence figure is not in the abstract; read the full text.
- **Same CEE dataset with ITQ "clinical checks" (Hyland, Shevlin, Ben-Ezra, Cloitre, Adamkovic, Błachnio, Preiss, … Karatzias 2026, PLOS ONE)**: 82.2% trauma-exposed. Standard ITQ: PTSD **7.2%**, CPTSD **12.0%**. With clinical checks: PTSD **3.9%**, CPTSD **5.9%**. The clinical checks gave "more plausible prevalence estimates" and stronger associations with suicidality and anxiety-depression. In the Czech survey, suicidality items were reportedly omitted for ethical reasons (search-snippet detail, unverified) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0360940](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0360940)
- **Poland, representative (N = 1,816)**: ITQ PTSD 5.3% and CPTSD 6.1%, falling to 2.4% and 3.5% with clinical checks (a 48.7% relative drop for CPTSD) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2684107](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2684107)
- **Serbia clinical (N = 199 psychiatric patients)**: CPTSD 25.7% vs PTSD 18.7% [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128)
- **Lithuania** (post-Soviet, N = 280 outpatients): the ICD-11 factor structure was supported, with PTSD, CPTSD and low-symptom classes [FOUNDATIONAL] — [Kazlauskas et al. 2018, DOI 10.1080/20008198.2017.1414559](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008198.2017.1414559)

### Inferences
- The "true" population prevalence depends heavily on the measurement method. Self-report screening without clinical checks roughly doubles CPTSD rates (12.0% → 5.9% in CEE). A Czech thesis citing prevalence should report both figures and say that ITQ results mean "probable" CPTSD.
- Marek Preiss (Czech co-author on both 2026 CEE papers) is a likely Czech contact point for the ITQ and the CEE dataset. This is inferred from the author lists; his affiliation was not verified here.

### Gaps
- The Czech-only prevalence figures from the CEE dataset were not extracted (full texts not accessible).
- No Czech clinical-sample CPTSD prevalence study was found.

## 3. Measurement: ITQ psychometrics, Czech/Slovak versions, ACE

### Takeaway
The ITQ (12 items, freely available) is the standard self-report measure and has many validations, including Polish 2025, Serbian 2026 and German IRT studies. A Czech version has been used in the 2026 CEE studies, and a Czech master's thesis validation exists (Sokol, theses.cz). No peer-reviewed standalone Czech ITQ validation paper was found. A Czech CTQ (Childhood Trauma Questionnaire) validation exists, and so does a Slovak CTQ one.

### Cited Findings
- ITQ development: IRT on a UK community sample (n = 1,051) and a clinical sample (n = 247) produced the optimized 12-item version. "The measure is freely available and can be found in the body of this paper." [FOUNDATIONAL] — [Cloitre et al. 2018, Acta Psychiatr Scand, DOI 10.1111/acps.12956](https://doi.org/10.1111/acps.12956)
- DSO item psychometrics [FOUNDATIONAL] — [Shevlin et al. 2018, DOI 10.1080/20008198.2017.1419749](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008198.2017.1419749)
- ITQ measures reliable and clinically significant treatment-related change — [PMC8221157](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8221157/) (only the title was seen in search)
- Polish ITQ adaptation (2025) reported good internal consistency and a two-factor second-order model — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2025.2468116](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/20008066.2025.2468116) (search-snippet level)
- Serbian ITQ (2026): the six-factor correlated model and the second-order PTSD–DSO model fit well; "the single-factor model of CPTSD was not supported." Good reliability and validity [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128)
- German ITQ IRT validation — [PMC9667225](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9667225/) (title only)
- "Clinical checks" version (ITQ-CC): follow-up questions that verify the respondent understood each item. In Poland, symptom endorsements fell 18.9–41.6% [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2684107](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2684107); CEE version — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0360940](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0360940)
- **Czech ITQ:** a master's thesis by Antonín Sokol, "Validizační studie překladu sebeposuzovacího dotazníku posttraumatické stresové poruchy a komplexního traumatu na české populaci", is listed on theses.cz. The full text was not accessible; this is search-snippet level and the university/year are unverified — [theses.cz/id/0umv38](https://theses.cz/id/0umv38?lang=en)
- **Czech CTQ:** psychometric analysis of the Czech Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, with sociodemographic differences in traumatization of Czech adults — [ResearchGate 329671651](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329671651_Psychometric_analysis_of_the_czech_version_of_childhood_trauma_questionnaire_CTQ_with_the_sociodemografic_differences_in_traumatization_of_czech_adults)
- **Slovak CTQ** validation — [PMC7967575](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7967575/)
- ACE questionnaires are used alongside the ITQ, e.g. ACE-Q in the Serbian validation and ACE-IQ in the adolescent study [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2646128); [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2685473](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2685473)

### Inferences
- A "pilot of a Czech ITQ translation" is not original anymore: a Czech translation exists (CEE studies, Sokol thesis). A **Czech ITQ-CC (clinical checks)** study, or a Czech **clinical-sample** validation, could still be new. A test–retest or convergent-validity study of the Czech ITQ against the Czech CTQ is another option.
- The student should contact the International Trauma Consortium (traumameasuresglobal.com) and/or the CEE authors (Preiss) to get the official Czech ITQ before building her own translation. That site was not checked in this session.

### Gaps
- traumameasuresglobal.com was not checked for an official Czech or Slovak ITQ listing.
- Czech ACE-Q/ACE-IQ validation: none found in this search. Unknown.

## 4. Treatment evidence (meta-analyses, RCTs, phase-based debate)

### Takeaway
Trauma-focused therapies (CBT, PE, EMDR) work for CPTSD symptoms, but effects are smaller with childhood-onset trauma. DBT-PTSD has the strongest RCT evidence for childhood-abuse CPTSD. A 2026 meta-analysis found phase-based and non-phase-based interventions mostly equivalent. The "stabilize first" debate (De Jongh et al. 2016) remains open, with evidence trending against mandatory stabilization but favouring multicomponent approaches for affect regulation.

### Cited Findings
- **Karatzias et al. 2019** (Psychological Medicine): 51 RCTs. CBT, exposure alone and EMDR were superior to usual care for PTSD symptoms (CBT g = −0.90, k = 27; EMDR g = −1.26, k = 4, low quality). CBT and exposure had moderate-large effects on negative self-concept, and there were moderate effects on disturbed relationships. "Few RCTs reported affect dysregulation data." "Childhood-onset trauma was associated with a poorer outcome." [CURRENT, 2019] — [DOI 10.1017/S0033291719000436](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291719000436)
- **Group treatments** meta-analysis for CPTSD symptoms (Mahoney, Karatzias et al. 2018) [FOUNDATIONAL] — [DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2018.09.059](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2018.09.059) (title only)
- **JAD 2025 meta-analysis (RCTs, adults exposed to complex trauma)**: PTSD g = −1.16 (k = 27), depression g = −1.12, anxiety g = −1.25, dissociation g = −0.47 (k = 7). Effects declined slightly at follow-up. The subgroup on childhood trauma was truncated in the retrieved abstract [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2025.03.153](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2025.03.153)
- **Phase-based vs non-phase-based meta-analysis (Lee, Park, Cho 2026, EJPT)**: "Across most outcomes, we observed no significant differences between phase- and non-phase-based interventions … or between exposure and non-exposure interventions." However, "multi-phase interventions had superior effects on PTSD", and phase-based/multi-phase/exposure interventions improved DSO affect regulation more. Limited by a small number of trials [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2644112](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2644112)
- **De Jongh, Resick, Zoellner et al. 2016**: research supporting phase-based treatment is "methodologically limited". There is no rigorous evidence that a stabilization phase is necessary or that trauma-focused treatment has unacceptable risks. Current guidelines "may therefore be too conservative, risking that patients are denied or delayed" [FOUNDATIONAL] — [DOI 10.1002/da.22469](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22469)
- **Prolonged exposure meta-analysis (Zhao et al. 2026)**: 43 RCTs, N = 4,964. PE vs inactive control g = 0.74. Complex-presentation samples g = 0.50 (k = 14) vs unselected samples g = 0.37, not significantly different, "imprecision … precludes conclusions of equivalence". PE showed no superiority over CPT or EMDR (g = 0.04) [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122612](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.122612)
- **DBT-PTSD vs CPT RCT (Bohus et al. 2020, JAMA Psychiatry)**: 193 women with childhood-abuse PTSD plus ≥3 BPD criteria. CAPS-5 d = 1.35 (DBT-PTSD) vs 0.98 (CPT), between-group d = 0.33. Dropout 25.5% vs 39.0%. Remission 58.4% vs 40.7% [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.2148](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.2148)
- DBT-PTSD gains held at 9 months, and DBT-PTSD stayed superior (d = 0.35 CAPS to 0.57 BSL-23/GAF) [CURRENT] — [Vonderlin et al. 2024, DOI 10.1080/20008066.2024.2393061](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2024.2393061)
- BPD + PTSD subgroup (n = 93): DBT-PTSD superior for PTSD, BPD and dissociation [CURRENT] — [Kleindienst et al. 2021, DOI 10.1037/ccp0000687](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000687)
- DBT-PTSD has outpatient (45 weeks) and residential (12 weeks) formats [CURRENT] — [Bohus & Vonderlin 2024, Nervenarzt, DOI 10.1007/s00115-024-01680-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00115-024-01680-y)
- **Dissociation**: across 13 controlled studies, treatments have a small effect on dissociation (g = −0.28). Treatments "need to focus on dissociation more explicitly" [CURRENT] — [Akoral et al. 2026, DOI 10.1080/15299732.2026.2641635](https://doi.org/10.1080/15299732.2026.2641635)
- **Lancet 2022 recommendation**: "multicomponent therapies starting with a focus on safety, psychoeducation, and patient-provider collaboration," including self-regulatory and trauma-focused components [CURRENT] — [Maercker et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(22)00821-2)
- **Youth component network meta-analysis**: 48 RCTs, 6,017 participants. Multimodal group SMD −1.68, phase-based TF-CBT with parents −1.12, TF-CBT −1.06 [CURRENT] — [Psychother Psychosom 2026, DOI 10.1159/pps/aemag009](https://doi.org/10.1159/pps/aemag009)
- **ISTSS**: the 2012 Expert Consensus Guidelines for Complex PTSD in Adults recommended phase-based treatment [FOUNDATIONAL] — [psychotraumanet listing](https://psychotraumanet.org/en/node/2101). The 2018–2019 ISTSS PTSD guidelines considered separate scoping questions on complex presentations but decided against them "due to definitional issues", and published position papers on CPTSD alongside (search-snippet level) — [ORCA Cardiff 120985](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/120985/); [EMDRIA summary](https://emdria.org/resource/ptsd-prevention-and-treatment-guidelines-methodology-and-recommendations-international-society-for-traumatic-stress-studies)

### Inferences
- The field has moved from "stabilize first" (ISTSS 2012) toward "flexible, multicomponent, trauma-focused care tailored to the patient." Lay and peer communities often still frame therapy as stabilization-first. That contrast is a possible misconception topic.
- Bachelor students cannot run RCTs. Treatment can only be a literature-review topic here, for example a structured review of the phase-based debate after 2019.

### Gaps
- **STAIR** (Cloitre), **ESTIES** (Karatzias), **schema therapy** and EMDR-specific CPTSD RCTs from 2023–2026 were not retrieved individually because of the tool-call budget. Search PubMed for "STAIR narrative therapy", "ESTIES", "schema therapy complex PTSD RCT" and "EMDR complex PTSD RCT".
- No ISTSS 2024–2026 CPTSD-specific guideline update was found; existence unknown.
- The Czech availability of DBT-PTSD/STAIR training was not researched.

## 5. Controversies and misconceptions (BPD, childhood trauma, lay "complex trauma", Pete Walker terms, dissociation)

### Takeaway
CPTSD and BPD are empirically distinguishable but strongly overlapping. The best differentiator is self-concept: stably negative in CPTSD, unstable in BPD. ICD-11 does not require childhood trauma. Popular terms such as "emotional flashbacks" and "fawn" (Pete Walker) are not ICD-11 criteria. No peer-reviewed study specifically auditing CPTSD social-media content was found; studies on PTSD TikTok show about a third of top content is misleading.

### Cited Findings
- **Cloitre et al. 2014** latent class analysis (280 women with childhood abuse) found separate PTSD, CPTSD, BPD and low-symptom classes. The BPD markers that set people apart from CPTSD were frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, unstable sense of self, unstable/intense relationships and impulsiveness [FOUNDATIONAL] — [DOI 10.3402/ejpt.v5.25097](https://doi.org/10.3402/ejpt.v5.25097)
- **Cyr et al. 2021** (community women, N = 438) found five profiles: high PTSD 12.0%, high CPTSD 7.6%, high BPD 9.9%, high CPTSD+BPD 3.8%, low 66.7%. These "support the distinction" [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1002/jts.22719](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.22719)
- **Gálvez-Merlín et al. 2026** systematic review (7 studies, N = 2,574): the two are "empirically distinguishable, though substantially correlated". Affective dysregulation is the central bridge. "Self-concept emerged as the most robust differentiator: stable and persistently negative in CPTSD versus unstable and fragmented in BPD." Shame marks severity. Caution because the studies were cross-sectional [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.3390/healthcare14162527](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14162527)
- About half of people seeking treatment for BPD present with co-occurring PTSD [CURRENT] — [Kleindienst et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000687)
- Network synthesis (22 articles, 46 networks): "feelings of failure" and "feelings of worthlessness" (negative self-concept) are the most central CPTSD symptoms [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103234](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103234)
- Latent profiles highlight "emotional hypoactivation" (affect dysregulation includes numbing/detachment as well as hyperactivation) [CURRENT] — [EJPT 2026, DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2661294](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2661294) (title only)
- Dissociation is "not being recognized by clinicians" and is often not targeted [CURRENT] — [Akoral et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15299732.2026.2641635)
- Therapists (N = 60): DSO was linked to impaired personality organization, ACEs and dissociation, and predicted therapist helplessness. Patient dissociation predicted therapist disconnection [CURRENT] — [Scalabrini et al. 2026, DOI 10.1002/cpp.70309](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.70309)
- **Self-report inflation (a methodological controversy):** clinical checks halve CPTSD prevalence estimates [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0360940](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0360940)
- **Social media:** about one-third of the 100 most-liked #PTSD TikTok videos contained misleading information. Accuracy was not associated with engagement. Self-identified health professionals produced 33% (4/12) of the misleading videos [CURRENT, 2025] — [PMC12422738](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12422738/)
- German-language TikTok mental health videos: about half contained incorrect information. PTSD videos and expert-made videos scored best [CURRENT] — [Clinical Psychology in Europe, PsychOpen](https://cpe.psychopen.eu/index.php/cpe/article/download/17279/17279.html?inline=1)

### Inferences
- **Misconception list** (needs checking against primary text for each item):
  1. "CPTSD = childhood trauma". Wrong: ICD-11 also covers adult domestic violence, torture, war and captivity. Prevalence in refugees and the military is high.
  2. "CPTSD is just BPD by another name". Not supported: the two are distinct, though they overlap and co-occur.
  3. "Complex trauma" (an exposure) is not the same as "complex PTSD" (a diagnosis). Many people exposed to complex trauma do not develop CPTSD.
  4. "Emotional flashbacks", "fawn response" and the "4F" model come from Pete Walker's clinical/self-help writing and are not ICD-11 criteria. No source was retrieved in this session, so a citation from Walker's book is needed.
  5. "You must stabilize for months before trauma work". This is contested and not supported as mandatory.
  6. "A high ITQ score means you have CPTSD". No: a screener gives probable caseness only, and clinical checks halve the rates.
- No CPTSD-specific social-media content analysis was found. That makes it a clear gap and a feasible bachelor topic.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved for Pete Walker's terms or for Ford & Courtois' position on BPD/CPTSD.
- No peer-reviewed study of Czech-language online cPTSD content was found.

## 6. Peer support, lived experience, post-traumatic growth, adult recovery trajectories

### Takeaway
The evidence here is thin and recent: small proof-of-concept peer-led programmes, conceptual recovery frameworks, and early network/correlational work on PTG. CPTSD-specific symptoms (DSO) correlate negatively with PTG, while core PTSD symptoms correlate positively. No RCT of peer support for ICD-11 CPTSD was found, which makes this a real gap.

### Cited Findings
- **Left Write Hook** (survivor-designed, peer-led expressive writing plus boxing, 8 weeks, n = 17 CSA survivors): significant pre–post decreases in self-reported PTSD, CPTSD, depression, anxiety and stress. Small sample, no control group [CURRENT, 2026] — [Veltman et al., DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2713962](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2713962)
- **Recovery Assemblage Framework for Trauma (RAF-T)**: a feminist participatory action research framework with adult survivors of childhood trauma in UK community settings. It critiques "linear pathways focused on symptom reduction" and frames recovery as relational and contextual [CURRENT, 2026] — [Christodoulides et al., DOI 10.3389/fpubh.2026.1868296](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1868296)
- **PTG network** (N = 704, two waves): "the majority of edges between PTSD symptoms and PTG were positive, whereas the majority of edges between CPTSD-specific symptoms and PTG were negative." Bridge symptoms included "difficulties feeling close to others" and "changed priorities" [CURRENT, 2026] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2658989](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2658989)
- **Social identity and DV survivors** (n = 64, Ireland): more community identities predicted lower complex PTS symptoms and higher PTG. More family identities predicted higher complex PTS symptoms [CURRENT, 2026] — [DOI 10.1037/tra0002201](https://doi.org/10.1037/tra0002201)
- **Co-creation with lived experience**: a bibliotherapy e-storybook for children of parents with CPTSD, co-created with more than 30 participants, including people with lived experience [CURRENT, 2026] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2732729](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2732729)
- Self-compassion has been proposed as a therapeutic target for ICD-11 CPTSD [FOUNDATIONAL] — [Karatzias et al. 2018, DOI 10.1017/S1352465818000577](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1352465818000577) (title only)
- Childhood-trauma trajectories review covering resilience, PTG, PTSD and personality disorder [CURRENT, 2026, Russian] — [DOI 10.17116/jnevro202612609234](https://doi.org/10.17116/jnevro202612609234)
- DBT-PTSD trial participants had a mean age of 36.3 (SD 11.1) years, so adults in their 30s–40s with childhood-abuse CPTSD can be treated effectively [CURRENT] — [Bohus et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.2148)

### Inferences
- Peer work with CPTSD survivors in CZ (e.g. peer consultants in Czech mental-health reform services) appears to be unstudied. This is inferred from no hits, not from a Czech database search.
- The negative link between DSO and PTG suggests a qualitative thesis question: how do adult survivors describe growth in self-concept and relationships, as opposed to symptom relief?

### Gaps
- No systematic review of peer support specifically for CPTSD was found.
- No longitudinal adult (30–50 years) CPTSD recovery-trajectory study was found within budget.
- No Czech literature on peer support in trauma was searched (Czech databases were not accessible).

## 7. Mental health literacy about PTSD/CPTSD (public and professionals)

### Takeaway
Clinicians in routine services know the criteria but struggle with differential diagnosis, building trust, and resources. Vignette studies show CPTSD improves diagnostic accuracy. No public-literacy study specific to CPTSD, and no Czech study, was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Ahern et al. 2026** (EJPT, interviews with 20 psychologists/psychiatrists in public adult mental health): themes were "C-PTSD is beyond a simple diagnosis" (difficulty with trauma history and differential diagnosis), "Balancing treatment on a tightrope" (trust before processing), and "Resourcing" (training, supervision, trauma-informed services). "While clinicians are aware of the diagnostic criteria, building a therapeutic relationship with C-PTSD clients remains a challenge." [CURRENT] — [DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2685933](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2685933)
- A 2025 scoping review of 11 qualitative studies reported that clinicians struggle with awareness and detection of C-PTSD and need tailored training. This is search-snippet level and the exact citation was not retrieved — see the [web search summary; University of Limerick repository](https://researchrepository.ul.ie/entities/publication/2ccfff58-da47-4e0a-8705-3a0cf3470281) (unverified match)
- An international study of mental health professionals found that recognizing CPTSD-specific features predicted correct diagnosis (search-snippet level, primary citation not confirmed) — [Elsevier IJCHP 2014 resolver](https://www.elsevier.es/doi-resolver-10.1016/j.ijchp.2014.03.003) [FOUNDATIONAL]
- A US dissertation surveyed 217 outpatient clinicians on their confidence identifying PTSD vs CPTSD — [Lesley University dissertation](https://digitalcommons.lesley.edu/counseling_dissertations/16)
- Dissociation is under-recognized by clinicians [CURRENT] — [Akoral et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15299732.2026.2641635)

### Inferences
- There is clearly no Czech study of CPTSD knowledge among psychology students or professionals. A vignette or knowledge survey is feasible at bachelor level and builds on an existing international method (ICD-11 field-study vignettes).

### Gaps
- No public (lay) mental-health-literacy study on CPTSD was found. PTSD literacy studies exist but were not retrieved.
- No Czech data on clinician use of ICD-11 trauma diagnoses was found.

## 8. Proposed bachelor-thesis directions (ALL = INFERENCE, grounded in the gaps above)

Feasibility rule of thumb for a Czech BP: N ≈ 100–300 for an online survey, 6–12 interviews for qualitative work, or a structured literature review. Ethics committee approval is needed for any trauma-related data. A lived-experience researcher should plan reflexivity and self-care, and avoid recruiting people from her own peer clients.

1. **Knowledge and misconceptions about ICD-11 cPTSD among Czech psychology students (and/or helping professionals)**: an online vignette plus knowledge quiz (PTSD vs CPTSD vs BPD vignettes, myth items such as "requires childhood trauma" and "fawn is a criterion"). *Gap:* no Czech study, and international work is mostly qualitative or on clinicians (Ahern 2026; vignette field studies). *Method:* cross-sectional survey, descriptive statistics, comparison by year of study. **Highly feasible.**
2. **Content analysis of Czech (and/or English) social media posts on "komplexní trauma / C-PTSD" versus ICD-11 criteria**: code the 100 most-viewed TikTok/Instagram/YouTube items for accuracy, use of non-ICD terms (emotional flashback, fawn), creator type, and engagement. *Gap:* existing studies cover #PTSD only, not CPTSD (PMC12422738; the German CPE study). *Method:* quantitative content analysis with two coders and inter-rater reliability (Cohen's κ), following the PMC12422738 protocol. **Highly feasible, no vulnerable participants.**
3. **Qualitative study of adult survivors (35–50 years) of childhood abuse: how they understand "recovery" and "growth"**: semi-structured interviews analysed with IPA or reflexive TA. *Gap:* PTG is negatively linked to DSO (EJPT 2026), RAF-T calls for non-linear recovery models, and there are no Czech data. *Method:* 6–10 interviews. Ethics: recruit outside her own peer caseload and have a referral plan ready.
4. **Peer workers' experience of supporting clients with cPTSD in Czech services**: interviews with peer consultants (peer konzultanti) about boundaries, triggers, and the value of lived experience. *Gap:* peer-led evidence is limited to small pilots (Left Write Hook 2026), and nothing exists for CZ. *Method:* qualitative TA, 6–10 participants. Fits her role well.
5. **Narrative/structured review: "Stabilize first?" The phase-based vs trauma-focused debate for CPTSD, 2016–2026**: synthesize De Jongh 2016, Karatzias 2019, Bohus 2020, Lee 2026, Zhao 2026 and ISTSS documents, and compare with what Czech treatment-guidance texts say. *Gap:* no Czech-language review exists, and the evidence shifted in 2026. *Method:* PRISMA-informed scoping review. **Feasible, low ethical risk.**
6. **CPTSD vs BPD: differential diagnosis and stigma**: either a literature review (Cloitre 2014, Cyr 2021, Gálvez-Merlín 2026) or a vignette experiment measuring how students' stigma and treatment optimism change when the same case is labelled "BPD" vs "CPTSD". *Gap:* the label effect on stigma was not found in this search. *Method:* between-subjects online experiment, N ≈ 150, t-test or ANOVA.
7. **Czech ITQ with "clinical checks" (ITQ-CC), or a convergent validation of the Czech ITQ with the Czech CTQ in a student or community sample**: *Gap:* a Czech ITQ exists (CEE 2026 studies, Sokol thesis), but there is no standalone peer-reviewed Czech validation and no Czech ITQ-CC check. *Method:* online survey N ≈ 200–300, Cronbach's α/ω, correlations, possibly CFA with a supervisor's help. **Feasible but statistically demanding.** Coordinate with the CEE authors (Preiss) first to avoid duplication.
8. **Self-labelling: people who self-identify as having "C-PTSD" online vs their ITQ (and ITQ-CC) profile**: a survey in Czech online communities asking for self-label, information sources, and the ITQ. *Gap:* the self-report inflation shown by clinical checks (PLOS ONE 2026) and social-media influence have not been combined in one study. *Method:* survey with descriptive and agreement statistics. *Caution:* ethically sensitive, so a crisis-resource protocol is needed.
9. **Dissociation as a "forgotten" CPTSD feature: what Czech practitioners know**: a short survey or interviews. *Gap:* dissociation is under-recognized and under-treated (Akoral 2026; Scalabrini 2026). *Method:* survey of psychologists or psychotherapists, or 8 interviews.

**Recommendation (inference):** the strongest options for originality versus feasibility are #1, #2 and #5. Each needs no clinical access and fills a clear Czech gap. #3 and #4 make best use of her lived and peer experience but require careful ethical design.

### Gaps (for this section)
- No search of Czech thesis repositories (theses.cz, blocked) was done to check whether topics #1, #2 and #6 have already been done as Czech BP/DP theses. **The student must check theses.cz and dspace.cuni.cz before choosing.**
